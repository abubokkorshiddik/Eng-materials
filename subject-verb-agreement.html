Subject–verb agreement

The verb must match its real subject, not the noun nearest to it.

Part 1: The core rule

In plain words

Agreement is a handshake between the subject and the verb. A singular subject takes a singular verb, a plural subject takes a plural verb. It sounds easy until the subject is hidden behind a long phrase, or a word like people, news or a number of looks like one thing but acts like another. The fix is always the same: find the real subject, decide singular or plural, then choose the verb.

How to build it

Singular one thing: is, was, has, does, works

Plural more than one: are, were, have, do, work

Present he/she/it + verb-s: The graph shows. The graphs show.

Shrink it Replace the subject with he, she, it or they, then pick the verb.

3 steps 1) Find the verb. 2) Ask “who or what?” to find the subject. 3) Shrink it, then choose.

Verb forms at a glance

be (present): I am, he/she/it is, we/you/they are. be (past): I/he/she/it was, we/you/they were.

have: I/we/you/they have, he/she/it has. do: I/we/you/they do, he/she/it does.

Other verbs (present simple): he works, they work. Past simple: no change (worked), except was/were.

Modals (can, will, must, should, may) never change: he can, they can. After do/does/did and modals, the main verb is the base form: Does he work? He can go.

You always takes plural forms, even for one person: you are, you were, you have. I takes am, was, have, work.

Spelling the -s form

work → works, show → shows, play → plays, buy → buys

pass → passes, watch → watches, fix → fixes, go → goes

study → studies, try → tries, carry → carries

Ends in s, sh, ch, x, z, o: add -es. Consonant + y: -ies. Vowel + y: just -s. Irregular: have → has, be → is.

Part 2: Finding the real subject

When we use it

The basic match

The student works hard. The students work hard.

My sister has a car. My sisters have cars.

The graph shows a rise. The graphs show a rise.

Singular third person takes -s in the present. The -s goes on the noun for plural, on the verb for singular, never both.

Phrases between subject and verb

The cost of new buildings has risen.

The results of the survey show a clear trend.

The manager, along with her team, is attending.

A box of chocolates was on the table.

“Of new buildings”, “of the survey” and “along with her team” are extra detail. The subject is cost, results, manager, box. Also ignore: in, on, with, for, from, as well as, together with, including, besides, accompanied by.

Two subjects joined by and

Reading and writing are tested in IELTS.

My teacher and my friend were there.

Bread and butter is my usual breakfast.

The founder and CEO has resigned. The founder and the CEO have resigned.

Every man and woman has a vote.

Two things joined by and take a plural verb. Exceptions: one idea (bread and butter, fish and chips), one person with two roles (no second “the”), and each/every before the pair.

Or, nor, either…or, neither…nor, not only…but also

Neither the teacher nor the students were ready.

Neither the students nor the teacher was ready.

Either Tom or his parents are coming.

Not only the students but also the teacher is tired.

The verb agrees with the nearer subject. If it sounds awkward, put the plural subject last.

There is / there are, here is / here are

There are three reasons for this.

There is a book and two pens on the desk.

Here are the results.

The real subject comes after there. Check the noun that follows. In writing, avoid “There’s” with plurals.

Inverted order and questions

Beside the house stand two trees.

Never have I seen such a view.

Where are the keys? What is the answer? Does she know? Do they know?

The verb still agrees with the subject, even when it comes first. Turn the question into a statement to check: “The keys are where?”

Groups

The committee has made its decision. The police are investigating.

The team is training today. The team are arguing among themselves.

Most group nouns act as one unit. Use plural only when members act separately (mostly British English). Police, people and children are always plural. Stay consistent in one sentence.

Gerunds, infinitives and clauses as subjects

Reading books is fun. To learn takes time. What he said was true.

Swimming and running are good exercise.

One gerund, infinitive or clause is singular, even with plural nouns inside it. Two joined by and are plural.

Part 3: Nouns, groups and numbers

Uncountable nouns are singular

The research is clear. The advice was helpful. The equipment is new. Traffic is heavy.

Information, advice, research, equipment, furniture, luggage, evidence, knowledge, progress, traffic, homework, weather, money, music, work, news, vocabulary, software, feedback, health. No -s and no plural verb: a piece of advice, some information, much evidence.

Singular words that end in -s

The news is good. Economics is difficult. Physics was my favourite. This species is rare. A series of events was planned.

News, mathematics, physics, economics, politics, athletics, linguistics, diabetes, measles, series, means, species. Statistics as a subject is singular. The statistics (figures) are plural.

Plural-only nouns

My trousers are new. A pair of trousers is on the bed. The clothes are dry. The goods were damaged. Thanks are due to everyone.

People, police, children, men, women, cattle, clothes, goods, belongings, savings, earnings, surroundings, glasses, scissors, jeans, shorts. With “a pair of”, the verb follows pair.

The number of, a number of and friends

The number of students is rising. A number of students are choosing this.

The amount of water is limited. A large amount of money was lost.

The range of options is wide. A range of options are available.

The proportion of women is high. The majority of people agree.

The rest of the work is easy. The rest of the books are here.

The + singular noun = singular. A number of, a variety of, a couple of, a range of = several = plural. The majority, the rest, the proportion: follow the noun after of.

Quantity words

Each of the books has a cover. Every student has a locker. Either answer is correct. Neither of them is late.

Both of the answers are correct. Several people were absent. Many problems exist. A few students are late.

Most of the water is clean. Most of the students are happy. Half of the cake is gone. Half of the cakes are gone.

Always singular: each, every, either, neither, one of, everyone, somebody, nothing. Always plural: both, several, many, few, a few, a couple of. Follow the noun after of: all of, most of, some of, any of, half of, a lot of, plenty of, a third of, 40% of.

Money, time, distance, measurement

Ten dollars is too much. Two weeks is a long time. Five miles is far. Three hundred grams is enough.

Fifteen per cent of the budget was spent. Fifteen per cent of the people were against it. Two-thirds of the land is farmland.

A single amount counts as one thing: singular. Percentages and fractions follow the noun after of.

Titles, names, countries

The United States is large. The Netherlands is flat. Friends was popular. “Brothers” is a plural noun.

Who, which, that and “one of the…”

She is one of the students who work late. She is the only student who works late.

One of the reasons is cost. He is a man who loves books.

The verb agrees with the noun the pronoun points to. In “one of the X who”, who = X (plural). With “the only one” it is singular.

Easy to confuse

Everyone is ready.
All of them are ready.

Everyone, everybody, someone, no one and each are singular.

The news is good.
The results are good.

News, economics, physics and mathematics end in -s but are singular.

The number of students is rising.
A number of students are choosing this.

The number of = singular. A number of = several = plural.

The police are here.
The policeman is here.

Police is always plural.

The statistics are alarming.
Statistics is a hard subject.

Figures are plural, the subject is singular.

Ten pounds is a lot.
Ten pound coins are in the box.

An amount is singular. Countable items are plural.

The majority of people agree.
The majority is in favour.

With of + plural noun, plural. Alone as a unit, singular.

Neither of the boys is here.
Neither boy is here.

Formally singular in both.

The data show a rise. The data shows a rise.
The datum is odd.

Data is plural in formal writing, often singular in everyday use. Pick one and keep it.

When in doubt, shrink the subject: replace it with he, she, it or they and see which verb fits.

Part 4: Mistakes people make

People is worried about prices. → People are worried about prices.

People is always plural.

The number of cars are increasing. → The number of cars is increasing.

Number is the subject, and it is singular.

A lot of people was present. → A lot of people were present.

Follow the noun after “a lot of”: people.

Everyone have a phone. → Everyone has a phone.

Everyone is singular.

The results of the study shows the problem. → The results of the study show the problem.

Results is the subject, not study.

There is many problems. → There are many problems.

Check the noun that comes after there.

Each of the students are late. → Each of the students is late.

Each is singular.

The information are useful. → The information is useful.

Uncountable nouns take singular verbs.

He don’t like it. → He doesn’t like it.

He/she/it takes doesn’t.

Does he works here? → Does he work here?

Does already carries the -s. The main verb stays in base form.

She can speaks three languages. → She can speak three languages.

No -s after modals.

The students was late. → The students were late.

Plural past of be is were.

The police is searching. → The police are searching.

Police is always plural.

Mathematics are difficult. → Mathematics is difficult.

A subject name ending in -s is singular.

Neither of them are ready. → Neither of them is ready.

Neither of is formally singular.

One of the reasons are cost. → One of the reasons is cost.

One is the subject.

Two years are a long time. → Two years is a long time.

A period of time is one amount.

My friends is coming. → My friends are coming.

Plural noun, plural verb.

The cause of the problems are unclear. → The cause of the problems is unclear.

Cause is the subject.

Smoking cigarettes cause cancer. → Smoking cigarettes causes cancer.

Smoking is the subject.

Where is my glasses? → Where are my glasses?

Glasses is plural.

The team have won its first game. → The team has won its first game.

Keep the verb and its pronoun consistent.

A variety of reasons is given. → A variety of reasons are given.

A variety of = several.

The proportion of women have risen. → The proportion of women has risen.

Proportion is the subject.

Both of them is correct. → Both of them are correct.

Both is plural.

He have finished. → He has finished.

Has with he/she/it.

Advices are useful. → Advice is useful.

No plural for uncountables.

The news were shocking. → The news was shocking.

News is singular.

Either of the answers are right. → Either of the answers is right.

Either of is singular.

The percentage of men are rising. → The percentage of men is rising.

Percentage is the subject.

The staff are meeting, and it is late. → The staff are meeting, and they are late.

Do not mix singular and plural for the same group.

Going deeper

Core test: find the head noun

Cross out any phrase beginning with of, in, with, along with, as well as or together with, and anything between commas. What is left is the subject. “The manager, along with her team, is attending.”

Uncountable nouns

Information, advice, research, equipment, furniture, evidence, knowledge, progress, traffic and news are uncountable and take singular verbs: The research is clear. Do not add -s to them. Use a piece of, an item of, a bit of to count them.

Quantity words

Singular: each, every, either, neither, one of, the number of. Plural: both, several, many, few, a number of, a couple of. Agrees with the noun after: all of, most of, some of, half of, a lot of. Most of the water is clean. Most of the students are happy.

None of

“None of the students was” and “none of the students were” are both used. Formal writing often prefers singular, everyday English plural. With uncountables it is always singular: None of the money is mine. Pick one and be consistent.

After who, which and that

The verb agrees with the noun the pronoun points to. “She is one of the students who work late” (who = the students). “She is the only student who works late.”

Titles and subjects of study

The United States is large. Mathematics is difficult. Names of books and films are singular: Friends was popular.

Tense and agreement

Present simple: he works, they work. Present perfect: he has worked, they have worked. Past continuous: he was working, they were working. Future and modals: never change. Passive: The report is written, the reports are written, the report was written.

Distance again

“The quality of the products that the company sells in its many shops across the country is high.” Shrink the subject to “the quality … is”. Long subjects are where most marks are lost.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • Shrink the subject. He, she, it → -s, is, has. They → no -s, are, have.
  • Look for “of”. In “the X of Y”, X is usually the subject.
  • People, police, children, men, women. Always plural.
  • Uncountable = singular. Information, advice, research, news.
  • The + number/amount/proportion = singular. A + number/variety = plural.
  • Each, every, -one, -body, -thing = singular.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “A number of” and “the number of”: The most tested pair in IELTS Writing Task 1.
  • “Neither of” and “either of”: Formally singular: Neither of them was late.
  • “One of the…”: One of the reasons is… One is the subject.
  • Inverted order: Beside the house stand two trees. The verb still agrees with trees.
  • Long subjects in Task 2: The increase in the number of private cars on our roads is a serious problem.
  • Percentages and fractions: 20% of the population is elderly, but 20% of the people are elderly.
  • Graph verbs: The figure rose. The figures rose. The proportion remained stable. The proportions remained stable.

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Find the subject before choosing the verb.
  • Treat people, police and children as plural.
  • Treat uncountable nouns as singular.
  • Reread every verb with its subject in Task 2.
  • Keep group nouns consistent.

Don’t

  • Don’t match the verb to the nearest noun.
  • Don’t write “informations” or “advices”.
  • Don’t say “people is”.
  • Don’t forget -s or -es with he, she, it.
  • Don’t add -s after does or a modal.

Why we get it wrong

Mother-tongue transfer

Many languages do not change the verb for the subject, so checking is not a habit.

Try this: Say subject and verb together: the cost has, the costs have.

Distance

A long subject pushes the verb far away, and the nearest noun takes over.

Try this: Underline the head noun and draw an arrow to its verb.

Words that look plural

News and economics end in -s, so they feel plural.

Try this: Keep a short list of the singular ones and check it when you write.

Speaking speed

When we speak fast, we drop the -s and say “he don’t”.

Try this: Slow down on he/she/it sentences and say the -s clearly.

Small habits that help

  • Underline every subject and verb in your last essay.
  • After writing, do one pass for -s endings, is/are and has/have only.
  • In Task 1, check every number, percentage and proportion phrase.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: watch the he/she/it forms. My brother works, my parents work.
  • Writing: Task 1 phrases. The number of… has risen. A number of… have. The percentage of… is.
  • Reading: find the subject across long phrases to follow the meaning.
  • Listening: -s endings are weak. Listen for is/are and has/have instead.

Real world: emails and reports with correct agreement look careful and professional.

Agreement slips count against grammatical accuracy in Writing and Speaking. A few are normal, but repeated ones lower the score.

    Show answerThe news is bad.: news is singular.
  4. A number of students is absent.
    Show answerA number of students are absent.: a number of means several.
  5. The quality of the roads have improved.
    Show answerThe quality of the roads has improved.
  6. She don’t understand.

  7. Neither of the answers are correct.
    Show answerNeither of the answers is correct.

    Show answerThere are several options.

Say it another way

  1. Use “the number of”: students / rising.
    Show answerThe number of students is rising.
  2. Use “a number of”: students / choosing online courses.
    Show answerA number of students are choosing online courses.
  3. Use “the proportion of”: women / increased.
    Show answerThe proportion of women has increased.
  4. Use “a variety of”: jobs / available.
    Show answerA variety of jobs are available.
  5. Use “each of”: children / have a book.
    Show answerEach of the children has a book.
  6. Use “neither of”: answers / correct.
    Show answerNeither of the answers is correct.
  7. Use “along with”: the manager / the staff / attend.
    Show answerThe manager, along with the staff, is attending.
  8. Use “one of the”: reasons / cost.
    Show answerOne of the reasons is cost.

  1. The price of oil and gas (rise/rises) in winter.

Correct the paragraph

“The number of people who owns a car are growing. A number of reason explains this. Everyone want freedom, and the news about prices are often good. Neither the government nor the companies is ready.”

Show answerThe number of people who own a car is growing. A number of reasons explain this. Everyone wants freedom, and the news about prices is often good. Neither the government nor the companies are ready.

Speak and write

  • Say five sentences about your family using he/she/it and they. Check every verb ending.
  • Write six Task 1 sentences using the number of, a number of and the percentage of.
  • Write five sentences using each of, both of, neither of, most of and none of.
  • Write a 120-word paragraph about your town. Underline every subject and verb.
  • Record yourself for one minute on “my daily routine”. Listen for missing -s endings.

Making it stick

  • Pick one habit: after every paragraph, circle each subject and its verb and check they match.
  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Redo the quiz after a day, three days and a week.
  • Read examples aloud, then write three about your own life.
  • Before submitting any essay, do a “verb-only” read, from the last sentence to the first.
