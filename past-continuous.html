Past Continuous

1. What is the past continuous?

The past continuous describes an activity or situation that was in progress at a particular time in the past. It often provides background information while another event happens.

Core meaning: Look back at a moment in the past and imagine what was happening around that time. The past continuous presents an activity as ongoing at that moment.
Action in progress

At a particular past time

At 9 p.m., I was studying English.

The sentence focuses on the activity underway at 9 p.m., not on when the studying began or ended.

Background action

An event happens during it

I was studying when my friend called.

Studying was in progress; the call is presented as the event that happened during it.

Think of a video paused in the past

Imagine a video recorded yesterday at 8 p.m. If the video shows you reading, you could say, “At 8 p.m. yesterday, I was reading.” The tense describes what was happening at that point in time.

It does not necessarily mean that you finished reading, and it does not tell us exactly when the activity started or stopped.

Remember: Continuous does not simply mean “a long action.” It presents an activity as in progress from a particular viewpoint. Even a short activity can be described this way if the context supports it.

2. Forms and structure

The past continuous has two essential parts: the past form of be (was/were) and the -ing form of the main verb.

Subject + was/were + verb-ing

2.1 Positive statements

SubjectCorrect structureExample
Iwas + verb-ingI was reading.
He / She / Itwas + verb-ingShe was working.
Youwere + verb-ingYou were listening.
We / Theywere + verb-ingThey were playing.
Examples:
I was preparing for my IELTS examination.
She was reading a research article.
We were discussing the results.
They were travelling to Dhaka.

2.2 Negative statements

Subject + was/were + not + verb-ing
Full formContractionExample
was notwasn'tI wasn't sleeping.
were notweren'tThey weren't waiting.

Why? The negative word not comes after was or were. The main verb remains in its -ing form.

Correct: She wasn't studying.
Incorrect: She didn't studying.

2.3 Yes/no questions

Was/Were + subject + verb-ing?
Was I speaking too quickly?
Was he sleeping?
Were you listening?
Were they waiting outside?

Why? Move was or were before the subject. Do not add did to form a past continuous question.

2.4 Short answers

QuestionPositive answerNegative answer
Were you studying?Yes, I was.No, I wasn't.
Was she working?Yes, she was.No, she wasn't.
Were they waiting?Yes, they were.No, they weren't.

2.5 Wh- questions

Question word + was/were + subject + verb-ing?
What were you doing at 8 p.m.?
Where was she going?
Why were they laughing?
Who were you talking to?

2.6 Questions about the subject

When who or what is the subject of the question, normal subject-question word order applies.

Who was talking? — Someone was talking.
What was making that noise? — A machine was making that noise.

2.7 The verb “be” itself

For ordinary descriptions of a past state, use the past simple of be, not usually the past continuous.

Correct: I was tired yesterday.
Usually incorrect for this meaning: I was being tired yesterday.

However, being is possible when describing temporary behaviour: “He was being unusually rude.” Here, it means he was behaving rudely at that time.

3. Spelling rules for the -ing form

Accurate spelling matters in IELTS Writing. Apply these common patterns when adding -ing.

Drop final silent emakemakingThe final e is usually removed.
Drop ewritewritingWrite becomes writing.
Double final consonant in some short stressed patternsrunrunningThe final n is doubled.
Double final consonantsitsittingThe final t is doubled.

Important: Consonant doubling depends on spelling, syllable stress, and the variety of English. For example, travel is commonly travelling in British English and traveling in American English. Be consistent in formal writing.

4. Main uses of the past continuous

Use 1: An action in progress at a particular past time

At 7 a.m. yesterday, I was preparing breakfast.
This time last week, we were attending a workshop.
At midnight, the researchers were analysing the data.

Why use it? The speaker looks at a particular moment and describes an activity that was already in progress. The sentence does not necessarily say whether the activity was completed.

Use 2: An ongoing activity interrupted by another event

I was walking home when it started to rain.
She was cooking when the electricity went off.
We were taking the examination when the alarm rang.

The longer or background activity is often in the past continuous. The event that happens during it is often in the past simple.

Why? The continuous gives the situation already in progress; the simple presents the event that occurred.

This is a common pattern, not an absolute rule that every long action must use the continuous or every short action must use the simple.

Use 3: Two activities happening at the same time

While I was reading, my brother was watching television.
The students were writing while the teacher was explaining the task.

Both activities were in progress during an overlapping period.

Use 4: Background description in stories

The rain was falling, the wind was blowing, and people were hurrying home. Suddenly, a loud noise came from the street.

The continuous establishes the scene. The past simple can then introduce the next important event.

Use 5: Temporary situations around a past period

I was living with my uncle while I studied for my examination.
She was working in Rangpur at that time.

The emphasis is on a temporary situation around a past period. The past simple may also be correct if the speaker simply wants to report the situation as a fact.

Use 6: Repeated behaviour that feels irritating or surprising

He was always interrupting me during meetings.
She was constantly checking her phone while we were talking.

With words such as always, constantly, and continually, the past continuous can express irritation, criticism, or an unusual repeated pattern.

Compare: “He always interrupted me” is a more neutral description of a repeated past habit.

Use 7: An unfinished activity at the time under discussion

At 10 p.m., I was writing the report.

This tells us that the writing was in progress at 10 p.m. It does not tell us whether the report was finished later.

Do not overgeneralise: The past continuous is not simply the tense for every long action. Choose it when you want to show an activity in progress, a background situation, or overlapping actions.

5. “When” and “while”: how to use them

When and while often occur with the past continuous, but they do not automatically determine which tense you must use. The meaning of the sentence matters.

5.1 When + past simple or past continuous

I was sleeping when the phone rang.
When I arrived, she was studying.
When I was living in Dhaka, I travelled by bus every day.

When can introduce an event, a point in time, or a period. Therefore, the tense after when depends on what you mean.

5.2 While + continuous action

While I was studying, my sister was cooking.
I listened to music while I was cleaning my room.
While we were waiting, the bus arrived.

While emphasises a period during which something happens. It often introduces an ongoing activity, but a simple tense is also possible when the verb and meaning suit it.

5.3 Compare these patterns

SentenceMeaning
I was reading when he arrived.Reading was in progress at the time of his arrival.
When he arrived, I opened the door.His arrival happened; then I opened the door, or the events are presented in that sequence.
While I was reading, he was cooking.Both activities were in progress during an overlapping period.
I met her while I was travelling.The meeting occurred during the travel period.
Punctuation: If a sentence begins with a dependent time clause, a comma is commonly used before the main clause: “While I was studying, my friend called.” If the time clause comes second, the comma is usually unnecessary: “My friend called while I was studying.”

6. Past simple vs past continuous

This is one of the most important distinctions to understand. Both tenses refer to the past, but they present events differently.

Past simplePast continuousDifference
I read the report yesterday.I was reading the report at 8 p.m.The simple reports a past event; the continuous focuses on an activity in progress at a past time.
I cooked dinner.I was cooking dinner when he arrived.The simple presents cooking as a completed event; the continuous makes it background to the arrival.
She worked there for five years.She was working there when I met her.The simple reports a past period; the continuous focuses on her situation at a particular time.
They played football.They were playing football at 5 p.m.The second sentence specifies that the activity was in progress at 5 p.m.
He knocked on the door.We were having dinner.The first reports the event; the second describes the background activity.

6.1 Why not use the past continuous everywhere?

Because the continuous does not simply mean that an event happened in the past. It gives the reader a particular viewpoint: the activity was in progress around a past reference time.

Natural: I completed my assignment yesterday.
Different meaning: I was completing my assignment yesterday.

The first reports completion. The second emphasises the process and does not necessarily say that the assignment was completed.

6.2 A useful decision method

Choose past simple when…

  • You report a completed past event.
  • You narrate a sequence of events.
  • You describe a past fact, state, or habit.
  • You want to emphasise what happened or what was completed.

Choose past continuous when…

  • You describe what was happening at a past time.
  • You set the background to another event.
  • You show two activities overlapping.
  • You emphasise the process rather than completion.

6.3 The past continuous and past perfect continuous

At 8 p.m., I was studying.
At 8 p.m., I had been studying for two hours.

The first says that studying was in progress at 8 p.m. The second emphasises how long the activity had continued before that past reference point.

7. Stative verbs: why some verbs are not usually continuous

Stative verbs commonly describe thoughts, feelings, possession, senses, or states rather than activities. They are normally used in simple forms when they express these meanings.

MeaningCommon verbsNatural past form
Knowledge and beliefknow, believe, understandI understood the question.
Possessionown, possess, belongThe book belonged to her.
Feelings and preferenceslike, love, prefer, hateI liked the course.
Needs and wantswant, needShe wanted some help.
Many states of perceptionseem, appearThe situation seemed serious.

Incorrect and correct examples

Incorrect for the usual meaning: I was knowing the answer.
Correct: I knew the answer.
Incorrect for the usual meaning: She was wanting a new phone.
Correct: She wanted a new phone.

Some verbs can be stative or dynamic

The same verb may be continuous when its meaning changes.

Simple formContinuous formDifference
I thought you were right.I was thinking about the problem.Think means an opinion in the first sentence and an ongoing mental activity in the second.
The soup tasted salty.The chef was tasting the soup.The first describes flavour; the second describes an action.
I had a car.I was having lunch.Have expresses possession in the first and an activity in the second.
The material felt soft.She was feeling the fabric.The first describes a quality; the second describes an action.

These are common patterns, not an absolute list of verbs that can never appear in continuous forms. Meaning and context determine the correct choice.

8. Time expressions

These expressions frequently appear with the past continuous:

at 7 p.m. yesterday · at that moment · this time last week · while · when · all evening · all afternoon · from 2 to 4 p.m.

Examples

  • At 10 o'clock last night, I was revising grammar.
  • This time last year, she was preparing for university admission.
  • We were discussing the project all afternoon.
  • They were waiting from 2 p.m. to 4 p.m.
Do not memorise a word-to-tense rule blindly. Words such as when, while, and all evening do not automatically require the past continuous. Choose the tense that matches the meaning.

9. Common mistakes and how to correct them

IncorrectCorrectWhy?
I were studying.I was studying.Use was with I, he, she, and it.
They was waiting.They were waiting.Use were with you, we, and they.
He was cook dinner.He was cooking dinner.Use was/were + verb-ing.
She didn't studying.She wasn't studying.Use was/were + not + verb-ing for the past continuous negative.
Did you were sleeping?Were you sleeping?Put was/were before the subject; do not add did.
Were he reading?Was he reading?Use was with he.
I was study English.I was studying English.The main verb needs the -ing form.
They were play football.They were playing football.Use were + playing, not were + base verb.
I was knowing him.I knew him.Know normally describes a state.
While I was walking, suddenly I was falling.While I was walking, I suddenly fell.The past simple often presents the sudden event.
I was reading yesterday and finished the book.I read the book yesterday and finished it.Use the past simple when reporting completed events as completed.
At 8 p.m., I studied when he called.I was studying when he called.The continuous clearly presents the ongoing background activity.

Why these errors happen

  • Confusing auxiliaries: Past simple questions often use did; past continuous questions use was/were.
  • Forgetting -ing: The past continuous always requires an -ing form of the main verb.
  • Overusing the continuous: Not every past action needs to be presented as ongoing.
  • Ignoring meaning: The correct tense depends on whether you are reporting an event, showing a process, or giving background information.
  • Translating word-for-word: A structure that sounds logical in your first language may not be grammatical in English.
Self-correction routine: Find the subject, check whether the auxiliary should be was or were, check the -ing form, and then ask whether the sentence really needs a continuous meaning.

10. Using the past continuous in IELTS Speaking and Writing

10.1 IELTS Speaking Part 1

You can use the past continuous to explain what you were doing during a past period, when the question invites a past experience.

Question: What were you doing during your last holiday?
Answer: I was spending most of my time with my family. We were visiting relatives and exploring places near our hometown.

The continuous helps describe activities in progress during that period. If the question asks for a completed event, the past simple may be more suitable.

10.2 IELTS Speaking Part 2: telling a story

For a cue card about an unexpected event, use the past continuous to establish the background, then use the past simple for key events.

Sample response:
“I remember a day when I experienced an unexpected situation. I was travelling home by bus, and it was raining heavily. Most passengers were looking out of the windows when the bus suddenly stopped. The driver explained that a tree had fallen across the road. We waited for about thirty minutes before another route became available.”

Why this works: The continuous forms set the scene. The past simple presents the important events and completed actions. The past perfect in “a tree had fallen” places the tree's fall before the driver's explanation.

10.3 IELTS Writing Task 1: describing graphs

In Academic Task 1, use the past simple when reporting completed changes during a past period. The past continuous is not usually needed for ordinary static chart descriptions.

Usually suitable: The unemployment rate increased between 2010 and 2015.
Different emphasis: At the time the policy was introduced, the unemployment rate was increasing.

The second sentence uses the past continuous because it describes a trend that was already in progress when another event occurred. Do not use the continuous merely to make a Task 1 answer sound more advanced.

10.4 IELTS Writing Task 2

Task 2 essays usually focus on arguments, explanations, and general claims. Use the past continuous only when a specific past context or ongoing past activity is relevant.

Example: While local authorities were expanding public transport, many residents continued to rely on private cars.

This sentence describes a policy activity that was in progress while another situation continued. For general arguments, present simple is often more appropriate.

10.5 What examiners need to see

  • Accurate tense choice that reflects your intended meaning.
  • Correct auxiliary and main verb forms.
  • Natural use of a range of tenses when the task calls for them.
  • Clear relationships between events and time references.
IELTS warning: Using more complicated grammar does not automatically increase your band score. Accuracy, relevance, naturalness, and appropriate variety matter more than forcing a tense into every answer.

11. Applying the tense to all four IELTS skills

Speaking

Use the past continuous to describe the background of a story, an activity in progress, or simultaneous past actions.

Example: “I was preparing for my exam when my friend called.”

Practise saying the whole sentence smoothly, including contractions such as wasn't and weren't.

Writing

Use it when the meaning requires an ongoing past situation. Do not replace the past simple in every sentence.

Example: “At the time the new policy was introduced, researchers were monitoring infection rates.”

The time relationship is what justifies the tense.

Reading

Look for was/were + -ing. Identify the ongoing background activity and distinguish it from events narrated in the past simple.

Example: “The team was collecting samples when the storm began.”

Recognising the tense can help you follow the sequence of events in a passage.

Listening

Listen for was, were, contractions, and the -ing ending. These forms may be less obvious in fast speech.

Example: “We were waiting outside when she arrived.”

Use the surrounding context to identify which activity was already in progress.

Listening practice method

  1. Listen once for the overall meaning.
  2. Listen again and note every was/were + -ing phrase.
  3. Identify the time reference and any event that happened during the activity.
  4. Repeat the sentences aloud, copying the speaker's rhythm.

Reading practice method

  1. Underline the past continuous forms.
  2. Circle the past simple forms in the same paragraph.
  3. Ask what was already happening and what happened next.
  4. Summarise the sequence in your own words.

12. What to do and what to avoid

Do

  • Use was/were + verb-ing.
  • Use the tense for an activity in progress at a past time.
  • Use it to provide background to a story.
  • Check whether two activities overlapped.
  • Use past simple for events that move a story forward.
  • Check subject–auxiliary agreement.
  • Read your sentence aloud to check whether the meaning is natural.

Do not

  • Write did + was/were to form a past continuous question.
  • Forget the -ing form of the main verb.
  • Use was with every subject.
  • Use the continuous with every past action.
  • Assume that when always requires past simple.
  • Assume that while always requires past continuous.
  • Force the tense into IELTS answers where it is not needed.

A five-second tense check

1. Am I describing an activity in progress at a past time?
2. Is the structure was/were + verb-ing?
3. Does the sentence clearly communicate the intended time relationship?
4. Would the past simple be more accurate if I want to report a completed event?
