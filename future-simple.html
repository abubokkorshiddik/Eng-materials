1. Overview of the future simple

The future simple with will is a way of talking about future events, predictions, decisions, promises and other situations viewed from the present.

Subject + will + base form of the verb
Positive

Prediction

I think technology will change education.

The speaker predicts a future development.

Negative

Future expectation

The weather will not remain stable.

The speaker expects something not to happen or continue.

Question

Asking about the future

Will more people work remotely?

The speaker asks about a possible future situation.

Spontaneous decision

Decision made now

The phone is ringing. I will answer it.

The decision is made at the moment of speaking.

Essential rule: Use will followed by the base form of a verb. Do not add -s, -ed, -ing or to to the main verb immediately after will.

For example, she will go is correct, not she will goes. The modal auxiliary will does not change according to the subject.

2. Understand the meaning before choosing the tense

English does not have one single grammatical tense that covers every future meaning. Speakers use several structures, including will, be going to, the present continuous and the present simple.

The choice depends on what the speaker wants to communicate. A sentence about tomorrow can use different structures because the speaker may be making a prediction, describing an arrangement or referring to a timetable.

Prediction: I think it will rain tomorrow.

Existing intention: I am going to study tonight.

Arrangement: I am meeting my supervisor tomorrow.

Timetable: The train leaves at 8 a.m.

Time and meaning are not the same thing

A future time expression does not automatically require will. Similarly, a sentence can contain will without mentioning a specific future time.

With a time expression: I will call you tomorrow.

Without a time expression: I promise I will help you.

The second sentence refers to a future commitment even though no date or time is mentioned.

Learning strategy: First identify the meaning of the sentence. Then choose the grammatical form that communicates that meaning. This is more reliable than memorising time words alone.

3. Form and structure

Will is a modal auxiliary verb. It stays the same with I, you, he, she, it, we and they. The main verb always remains in its base form when it directly follows will.

3.1 Positive statements

Subject + will + base verb + rest of sentence
SubjectStructureExample
II will + workI will work tomorrow.
YouYou will + workYou will improve with practice.
HeHe will + workHe will attend the meeting.
SheShe will + workShe will finish the report.
ItIt will + workIt will probably rain later.
WeWe will + workWe will discuss the results.
TheyThey will + workThey will publish the findings.

Examples with different verbs

  • I will study English every day.
  • She will write the research proposal.
  • They will analyse the data carefully.
  • We will be ready by Monday.
  • The results will have important implications.
  • The new system will make the process easier.
The base form is the ordinary dictionary form of the verb: go, study, write, be, have, do, make. The verb be is especially important because it does not become is or are after will.

3.2 Negative statements

Subject + will not + base verb
Subject + won't + base verb

Will not is the full form. Won't is its standard negative contraction. Both express a negative meaning, although the full form may receive more emphasis in some contexts.

Full formContracted formExample
I will notI won'tI won't forget your advice.
You will notYou won'tYou won't need to wait long.
He will notHe won'tHe won't attend the conference.
She will notShe won'tShe won't accept the offer.
It will notIt won'tIt won't take much time.
We will notWe won'tWe won't change the original data.
They will notThey won'tThey won't arrive before noon.

Meaning and emphasis

Ordinary negative: I won't attend the event.

Emphatic refusal: I will not accept those conditions.

The second sentence may express stronger determination or refusal, depending on the context and stress.

Remember that won't is not formed by adding not after the main verb. The negative word belongs with the auxiliary: will not.

3.3 Negative meaning with other words

A negative sentence does not always need won't. Words such as never, nobody and nothing can express negative meanings, although their placement and grammatical role differ.

I will never forget this experience.

Nobody will know the answer until the results are released.

Nothing will change unless we take action.

Avoid double negatives in standard English. For example, use “I won't do anything” or “I will do nothing”, rather than “I won't do nothing”.

3.4 Yes or no questions

To make a question, move will before the subject. Do not add do or does to form an ordinary question with will.

3.6 Short answers

In short answers, use will or won't instead of repeating the entire sentence.

Will you attend? Yes, I will. / No, I won't.

Will she come? Yes, she will. / No, she won't.

Will they help us? Yes, they will. / No, they won't.

Avoid “Yes, I will do” as the ordinary short answer to “Will you do it?” The natural answer is “Yes, I will.” The full verb may be repeated when the context requires it, but it is not needed in the usual short answer.

3.7 Negative questions

Won't + subject + base verb?

Negative questions may express surprise, expectations, concern or a request for confirmation.

Won't you join us for dinner?

Won't the new system solve this problem?

Won't they need additional training?

The exact meaning depends on context. A negative question can suggest that the speaker expected a positive answer, but this is not always so.

IELTS listening tip: Practise recognising I'll, you'll, he'll, she'll, we'll, they'll and won't. Focus on the meaning of the complete sentence rather than trying to hear every word separately.

4. The main uses of will

Will has several related uses. The same grammatical form can communicate different meanings depending on the situation, intention and context.

4.1 Predictions and opinions about the future

Use will when predicting what may happen or expressing a belief about the future. A prediction is not necessarily a certainty.

I think online education will become more accessible.

Many researchers believe temperatures will continue to rise.

In my opinion, public transport will play a greater role in urban life.

Perhaps people will spend less time commuting in the future.

Common expressions used with predictions

  • I think ...
  • I believe ...
  • I expect ...
  • In my opinion ...
  • Perhaps ...
  • Probably ...
  • It is likely that ...
  • I doubt that ...

I think renewable energy will become more affordable.

Renewable energy will probably become more affordable.

It is likely that renewable energy will become more affordable.

These sentences express similar predictions through different grammatical structures and degrees of emphasis.

Prediction versus certainty

Will often expresses an expectation, not an absolute guarantee. When discussing uncertain outcomes, use cautious language where appropriate.

Too certain without sufficient evidence: This policy will eliminate all poverty.

More cautious: This policy may reduce poverty.

Prediction with a qualification: This policy will probably reduce poverty if it is implemented effectively.

May, might and could express possibility. Will generally presents an outcome as the speaker's expectation or prediction. The best choice depends on the evidence and intended degree of confidence.

4.2 Predictions based on evidence

Both will and be going to can express predictions. However, be going to is often preferred when the prediction is based on visible or present evidence.

Evidence-based prediction: Look at those clouds. It is going to rain.

General prediction: I think it will rain tomorrow.

This is a common tendency, not an absolute rule. Will can also describe evidence-based predictions, and be going to can express a general expectation.

4.3 Spontaneous decisions

Use will when deciding to do something at the moment of speaking, rather than describing a plan already made.

The phone is ringing. I'll answer it.

That bag looks heavy. I'll carry it for you.

I am thirsty. I'll get some water.

We have no milk. I'll buy some on my way home.

Why this matters

Compare a decision made now with a prior intention.

SituationExampleReason
Decision now I'll order the chicken. The decision is made while ordering.
Prior intention I'm going to order the chicken. The speaker already has an intention.
Arrangement I'm meeting my friend at seven. The meeting is arranged.

4.4 Promises and commitments

Will is commonly used to make promises, assurances and commitments.

I will always support you.

I will send you the document by Friday.

We will protect the confidentiality of the data.

I won't tell anyone your secret.

In academic or professional settings, use promises carefully. A commitment should be realistic and consistent with the speaker's responsibilities.

4.5 Willingness and unwillingness

Will can express a person's willingness to do something. Won't can describe unwillingness or refusal, although it may also describe an ordinary prediction of non-occurrence.

Willingness: I will help you revise the report.

Unwillingness: He won't listen to any advice.

Refusal: She won't sign the agreement.

Prediction: The old computer won't run this software.

Context determines whether won't describes a person's refusal or an expected result. For example, “The computer won't start” usually describes a technical problem rather than a conscious decision.

4.6 Offers of help

Use will to offer assistance or volunteer to do something.

I'll open the window for you.

I'll help you prepare for the interview.

We'll carry the equipment upstairs.

These sentences are especially natural when the offer is made in response to a situation.

4.7 Requests and polite questions

Will you ...? can be used to make a request. The level of politeness depends on context, tone and the relationship between the speakers.

Will you close the door, please?

Will you send me the details this afternoon?

Will you wait here for a moment?

Could you ...? and Would you ...? can sound more polite or less direct in many situations.

ExpressionExampleTypical use
Will you ...?Will you help me?Direct request
Would you ...?Would you help me?Often more tentative or polite
Could you ...?Could you help me?Polite request about ability or willingness

4.8 Future facts and expected events

Will can state what is expected to happen in the future, including scheduled outcomes, expected results and anticipated events.

The examination results will be available next Monday.

The conference will begin with an opening address.

The new library will accommodate more students.

Our research team will present the findings in December.

For fixed timetables and official schedules, the present simple is often more conventional. Will is appropriate when presenting an event as a future expectation or outcome.

4.9 Threats, warnings and determination

Depending on context and intonation, will can express a warning, determination or a strong commitment.

You will regret making that decision.

I will finish this project, no matter how difficult it becomes.

We will not tolerate discrimination.

These are context-dependent uses. Will does not automatically create a threat or strong promise; the intended meaning comes from the sentence and the situation.

4.10 Consequences and logical expectations

Will can describe an expected consequence of a current situation.

If you leave ice in the sun, it will melt.

Without adequate preparation, students will find the test difficult.

Better data quality will improve the reliability of the analysis.

For scientific statements, distinguish between a general relationship, a conditional prediction and an established fact. The appropriate tense depends on the claim you are making.

5. Compare will with other future forms

These forms sometimes describe the same future event, but they can present different viewpoints. Learn the typical distinctions without assuming that any one form has only a single use.

5.1 Will versus be going to

WillBe going toTypical distinction
I think it will rain. Look at the clouds. It is going to rain. General prediction versus prediction based on present evidence.
I'll call her now. I'm going to call her tonight. Decision made now versus prior intention.
I will help you. I'm going to help you. May present a commitment versus an existing intention.
Remember: These distinctions are tendencies. Both forms can be used for predictions and intentions, and the wider context may matter more than the form alone.

5.2 Will versus the present continuous

The present continuous often describes a personal arrangement that has already been made.

ExampleLikely interpretation
I'm meeting my supervisor tomorrow. A meeting has been arranged.
I'll meet my supervisor tomorrow. A decision, promise or future expectation, depending on context.
We're travelling to Dhaka next week. A planned or arranged trip.
We'll travel to Dhaka next week. A future statement, prediction or commitment.

5.3 Will versus the present simple

The present simple commonly describes timetables, programmes and scheduled events.

The train leaves at 6:30 tomorrow morning.

The conference begins on Monday.

The semester ends in December.

Will is also possible when the speaker wishes to express an expectation rather than simply report the official schedule.

The train will leave shortly.

The conference will begin with a welcome speech.

5.4 Will versus the future continuous

Future simpleFuture continuousDifference
I will study at eight. I will be studying at eight. The second emphasizes an activity in progress at that time.
Will you use the office tomorrow? Will you be using the office tomorrow? The second can sound more neutral when asking about an expected activity.
She will work tomorrow. She will be working tomorrow. The second emphasizes the ongoing activity or expected situation.

5.5 Will versus the future perfect

Future simple: I will finish the report tomorrow.

Future perfect: I will have finished the report by tomorrow.

The future perfect looks back from a future point and presents the action as completed by that point.

5.6 Will versus may, might and could

SentenceTypical meaning
It will rain tomorrow.The speaker predicts rain.
It may rain tomorrow.Rain is a possibility.
It might rain tomorrow.Rain is a possibility, often expressed tentatively.
It could rain tomorrow.Rain is one possible outcome.

There is no universal percentage attached to each modal. Their perceived strength depends on context, speaker intention and the type of statement.

5.7 Quick comparison guide

Choose will when

  • You make a spontaneous decision.
  • You offer help or make a promise.
  • You express a prediction or expectation.
  • You ask about or state a future outcome.

Consider another form when

  • You describe an existing intention.
  • You report an arranged meeting.
  • You report a fixed timetable.
  • You need to emphasize duration or completion.

6. Time expressions commonly used with will

Time expressions can help locate a future event, but they do not determine the tense by themselves.

Adverbs of probability

Probably, possibly, certainly and perhaps can modify the statement by expressing the speaker's attitude towards the expected outcome.

She will probably arrive early.

They probably won't accept the proposal.

Perhaps the situation will improve.

The new policy will certainly attract attention.

In ordinary statements, probably usually comes after will and before the main verb. With a negative contraction, it often appears before won't or after won't, depending on the intended emphasis and variety of English.

She probably won't attend.

She won't probably attend is generally less natural in standard usage.

Avoid memorising time expressions as rigid tense rules. For example, “tomorrow” can occur with will, be going to, the present continuous or the present simple.

7. Will in future time clauses

A time clause tells us when something happens. When a time clause refers to the future, English normally uses the present simple in that clause rather than will.

Main clause: will + base verb
Time clause: present simple

7.1 The word when

Correct: When I arrive, I will call you.

Incorrect: When I will arrive, I will call you.

The first clause refers to a future arrival, but its verb is in the present simple. The second clause expresses the expected future action.

7.2 Other time conjunctions

ConjunctionExample
WhenWhen the results arrive, we will review them.
AfterAfter she finishes, she will submit the form.
BeforeBefore you leave, I will give you the documents.
As soon asAs soon as I hear from them, I will let you know.
UntilI will wait until the meeting ends.
OnceOnce the data arrive, we will begin the analysis.
By the timeBy the time you arrive, I will have finished.
WhileWhile you are away, I will look after the office.

7.3 Why the present simple is used

The time conjunction already establishes the future time relationship. The present simple is the standard form in many future time clauses. The main clause can then express the future action.

I'll contact you when I get home.

We'll start as soon as everyone arrives.

She will tell us after she speaks to the manager.

7.4 Important distinction: indirect questions

Do not confuse a future time clause with an indirect question. Indirect questions can use will when asking about a future event.

Time clause: When he arrives, we will begin.

Indirect question: I don't know when he will arrive.

In the first sentence, when introduces the time at which an action will happen. In the second, when introduces the information the speaker does not know.

Exam technique: Identify the grammatical function of when, before changing the verb. If it introduces a future time clause, the present simple is usually required. If it introduces an indirect question about the future, will may be correct.

8. Will in conditional sentences

Conditional sentences describe a condition and its possible or expected result. Will is especially common in the result clause of first conditional sentences.

8.1 First conditional

If + present simple, subject + will + base verb

If it rains tomorrow, we will stay at home.

If you study consistently, you will improve.

If the government invests in prevention, public health may improve.

The condition is presented as a realistic future possibility. The result clause expresses what the speaker expects or predicts if the condition is met.

8.2 The order can change

If I finish early, I will call you.

I will call you if I finish early.

When the if-clause comes first, it is normally followed by a comma. When the main clause comes first, the comma is usually unnecessary.

8.3 Do not normally use will in the if-clause

IncorrectCorrectReason
If it will rain, we will stay home. If it rains, we will stay home. The if-clause normally uses the present simple for a realistic future condition.
If she will arrive early, we will start. If she arrives early, we will start. Present simple expresses the future condition.

This is a useful general rule, but it is not absolute. Will can occur in an if-clause when it expresses willingness or a deliberate choice, rather than simply marking future time.

If you will wait here, the assistant will help you shortly.

Here, will can express willingness or polite cooperation. The sentence is more formal and less common than “If you wait here ...”.

8.4 Unless

Unless means “if not”. It commonly introduces a negative condition, while the main clause can contain will.

Unless we act now, the problem will become worse.

Unless you leave early, you will miss the bus.

Avoid adding another negative if it would reverse the intended meaning. “Unless you leave now” already means “if you do not leave now”.

8.5 Zero conditional versus first conditional

Zero conditionalFirst conditional
If water reaches 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure, it boils. If the water reaches 100°C, it will boil.
Describes a general fact or regular result. Describes an expected result in a particular possible situation.

Scientific statements need precise wording. A general law is often expressed with the present simple, whereas a prediction about a particular future situation can use will.

9. Advanced patterns with will

9.1 Will be

Will be is the future simple form of be. It is followed by an adjective, noun phrase or other complement.

The examination will be difficult.

She will be a valuable member of the team.

The findings will be useful for policymakers.

They will be ready by Monday.

9.2 Will have

Have can be the main verb after will. Do not confuse this with the future perfect, where will have is followed by a past participle.

Ordinary future: We will have enough time.

Future perfect: We will have completed the task by noon.

9.3 Will be able to

Use will be able to to express future ability. The modal can does not normally follow will directly.

Correct: I will be able to attend tomorrow.

Incorrect: I will can attend tomorrow.

9.4 Will have to

Will have to expresses future necessity or obligation.

We will have to submit the application before Friday.

Researchers will have to consider possible sources of bias.

Notice the difference between will have and will have to. The first can mean possession or refer to an action depending on the following words. The second expresses necessity.

9.5 Will need to

Students will need to practise regularly.

The research team will need to verify the data.

Need to expresses a requirement or necessity. It is followed by a base verb after to.

9.6 Will probably, definitely and possibly

The results will probably support the hypothesis.

The new policy will definitely require additional funding.

The programme will possibly attract international students.

Probably and definitely are natural in many positions around the auxiliary and main verb. Possibly is often more natural at the beginning or in expressions such as “It is possible that ...”.

9.7 Will still, already and no longer

Many people will still depend on public transport.

The revised system will already be in use by then.

Some outdated methods will no longer be necessary.

These expressions help clarify continuity, completion or change in a future situation. Choose the position that sounds natural for the particular adverb and sentence.

9.8 Will tend to

Will tend to describes an expected pattern or a likely general behaviour. It can be useful when a statement should not be presented as universal.

People will tend to adopt technologies that save time.

Demand will tend to increase during periods of extreme heat.

For research writing, make sure a tendency is supported by evidence. Avoid using will tend to as a substitute for an actual statistical result.

9.9 Shall and will

Shall appears in some questions and formal expressions, especially with I and we. In contemporary English, will is much more common for ordinary future statements.

Shall we begin?

Shall I open the window?

We will begin at nine.

Shall I ...? and Shall we ...? often ask for suggestions or offer to do something. In legal and contractual writing, shall can have specialised uses, so interpret it according to the document and jurisdiction.

9.10 Will in reported speech

When reporting what someone said in the past, will often changes to would if the reporting perspective moves into the past.

Direct speech: She said, “I will return tomorrow.”

Reported speech: She said that she would return the following day.

This is a common backshift pattern, not a rule that applies in every context. Will may remain when the future event is still future from the speaker's current perspective or when the original meaning is being preserved.

9.11 Will in passive sentences

Subject + will + be + past participle

The report will be published next month.

The data will be analysed using statistical models.

New guidelines will be introduced next year.

Use the passive when the action or result is more important than the person performing it. In academic writing, identify the agent when doing so is important for accuracy.

9.12 Will with a future perfect or continuous form

The following are different future constructions. They are included here so that you can recognise the limits of the future simple.

StructureExampleMain emphasis
Will + base verb I will write the report. Future action or outcome.
Will be + -ing I will be writing the report at noon. Action in progress at a future time.
Will have + past participle I will have written the report by noon. Completion by a future time.
Will have been + -ing By noon, I will have been writing for three hours. Duration up to a future point.

10. Common mistakes and why they happen

The following errors are common among English learners. Read the explanation, not just the correction, so that you can apply the same rule to unfamiliar sentences.

IncorrectCorrectExplanation
She will goes home. She will go home. Will takes the base form, without -s.
He will went there. He will go there. Do not use the past form after will.
I will to study. I will study. Do not insert to between will and the main verb.
She will studying. She will study. The future simple uses the base form, not -ing.
Will you can come? Will you be able to come? Use be able to after will when expressing future ability.
Does she will come? Will she come? Do not add do or does to an ordinary will question.
When I will arrive, I will call. When I arrive, I will call. Use the present simple in a future time clause.
If it will rain, we will stay home. If it rains, we will stay home. Use the present simple for a normal first conditional if-clause.
I will not goes. I will not go. Negation does not change the base form after will.
He won't to help. He won't help. Won't is followed directly by the base form.
I think she will comes. I think she will come. Will stays unchanged for every subject.
Will they arrives soon? Will they arrive soon? Use the base form in questions too.
The train will leaves at six. The train leaves at six. Use the present simple for the ordinary timetable statement.
I am agree that it will work. I agree that it will work. Agree is a verb, not an adjective requiring am.
The results will be publish. The results will be published. Passive voice requires be plus the past participle.
I will have finish by Friday. I will have finished by Friday. Future perfect requires the past participle after have.

Errors involving meaning rather than form

Mistake 1: Using will for every future event

Less suitable: I will meet my supervisor at 10 tomorrow, when describing an already arranged appointment.

Often more natural: I am meeting my supervisor at 10 tomorrow.

Will is not grammatically impossible in every such context. The present continuous simply communicates an existing arrangement more directly.

Mistake 2: Confusing an intention with a spontaneous decision

Existing plan: I am going to apply for the scholarship.

Decision now: I will apply for the scholarship.

The distinction is useful, but context can allow either form. Do not treat it as an absolute rule.

Mistake 3: Using will after every form of when

Time clause: I will tell you when I arrive.

Indirect question: I don't know when I will arrive.

Determine whether when refers to the time of an action or introduces a question about the time.

Mistake 4: Making unsupported predictions in academic writing

Overstated: This intervention will solve the entire problem.

More cautious: This intervention may reduce the problem if it is implemented effectively.

Choose the modal that matches the strength of your evidence. Grammar accuracy includes expressing uncertainty appropriately.

Mistake 5: Using future simple for an action in progress

General future: I will work tomorrow.

Activity in progress: I will be working at noon tomorrow.

The future continuous is more precise when the ongoing nature of the activity matters.

Self-editing checklist

  • Have I used the base verb after will or won't?
  • Have I formed questions by placing will before the subject?
  • Have I avoided unnecessary do or does?
  • Have I used the present simple in ordinary future time clauses?
  • Have I used the correct conditional structure?
  • Does the chosen future form communicate my intended meaning?
  • Is my prediction appropriately cautious for the evidence available?

11. Use will in IELTS speaking

Will is especially useful in IELTS Speaking Part 1 when talking about future plans, Part 2 when describing future expectations, and Part 3 when predicting social or technological changes.

11.1 Part 1: Personal predictions and intentions

Question: Do you think you will continue studying in the future?

Sample answer: Yes, I think I will continue studying because I want to develop my research skills and improve my academic English.

Question: Will you travel more in the future?

Sample answer: I hope I will have more opportunities to travel, particularly to places with interesting cultures and research institutions.

11.2 Part 2: A future goal

Possible cue card: Describe something you hope to achieve in the future.

In the future, I hope I will become a more confident researcher and develop the skills needed to contribute to meaningful scientific work. I will continue improving my English, learning new analytical methods and working on research projects. I believe these efforts will create better opportunities for my professional development.

This is a model for grammar practice. Adapt the ideas to your own experience rather than memorising a complete answer.

11.3 Part 3: Predictions about society

Question: How will technology change education?

Sample answer: I think technology will make education more accessible, particularly for students who live far from major educational institutions. However, access to reliable internet and digital devices will remain important.

Question: How will cities change in the future?

Sample answer: Cities will probably become more dependent on digital services and public transport. Nevertheless, whether these changes improve quality of life will depend on planning, investment and how equitably the benefits are distributed.

11.4 Useful speaking patterns

  • I think ... will probably ...
  • In the future, I expect that ... will ...
  • One possible outcome is that ... will ...
  • If this trend continues, ... will ...
  • I hope I will be able to ...
  • I believe this will make it easier to ...
  • It is difficult to predict exactly what will happen, but ...
Speaking tip: Answer the question directly, explain your reason, and give an example. Using will correctly is more important than forcing it into every sentence.

12. Use will in IELTS writing

Will can express future predictions, expected consequences, proposals and possible outcomes. In academic writing, however, a prediction should not be presented as a proven fact unless the evidence justifies it.

12.1 IELTS Writing Task 1: Future projections

For a graph containing projections, use the tense and wording that accurately reflect the time period and the source's level of certainty.

Example: The proportion of urban residents is projected to increase by 2040.

Alternative: The proportion of urban residents will increase by 2040.

The first explicitly presents the change as a projection. The second makes a direct prediction and may sound more certain.

12.2 IELTS Writing Task 2: Predictions and consequences

Topic: The growth of online education.

Online education will probably become more widely available as digital infrastructure improves. This development may reduce geographical barriers to learning, although unequal access to technology will remain a significant challenge.

Notice that the paragraph uses will probably for a prediction and may for a possible consequence. This avoids presenting every future outcome as certain.

12.3 First conditional in argumentative writing

If governments invest in preventive healthcare, they will have more opportunities to reduce avoidable illness and improve population health.

The conditional sentence explains a possible relationship between a policy and an expected outcome. It does not, by itself, prove that the outcome will occur.

12.4 Formal alternatives

ExpressionExampleWhen useful
WillThe policy will affect demand.Direct prediction or expected result.
Is likely toThe policy is likely to affect demand.Prediction expressed with qualification.

Is expected toDemand is expected to rise.Reported expectation or projection.
Is projected toDemand is projected to rise.Forecasts and numerical projections.

12.5 Common academic writing errors

  • Using will in every sentence about the future.
  • Presenting an uncertain projection as a guaranteed outcome.
  • Using will after when or if without considering the clause structure.
  • Repeating the same prediction structure in every sentence.
  • Using complex grammar that obscures the main argument.
Writing principle: Accuracy, clarity and logical development matter more than the number of advanced grammatical structures you use.

13. Recognise will in IELTS reading

In reading passages, will can signal an author's prediction, a forecast, a stated consequence or a commitment. Determine whether the statement is a fact, a possibility or an expectation.

Practice passage

Many cities are investing in public transport to reduce congestion. If these investments are accompanied by effective planning, they will improve access to employment and education. However, the benefits will not be distributed equally unless services are designed to reach underserved communities. Experts expect that future transport systems will rely more heavily on integrated digital services, although the speed of this transition remains uncertain.

Reading questions

  1. Which sentence uses will to express a conditional consequence?
  2. What does “will not be distributed equally” predict?
  3. Does the passage present the transition to digital services as certain in its timing?
  4. Which phrase explicitly attributes the prediction to experts?
Reveal the reading answers
  1. The sentence beginning “If these investments are accompanied by effective planning ...”.
  2. It predicts that the benefits will be unequal unless services reach underserved communities.
  3. No. The passage says the speed of the transition remains uncertain.
  4. “Experts expect that ...”.
Reading tip: Pay attention to limiting language such as unless, although, probably and may. These words can change the scope or certainty of a prediction.

14. Recognise will in IELTS listening

In natural speech, will is often contracted and unstressed. The following examples are useful for practising the grammar you may hear in dialogues and announcements.

14.1 Spoken examples

I'll send you the address later.

We'll probably arrive before noon.

They won't be able to attend the meeting.

When the speaker arrives, the session will begin.

14.2 Listening practice without audio

Read each line aloud or ask a study partner to read it while you write what you hear. Then check the spelling of the contraction and the verb form.

  1. I'll meet you outside the library.
  2. We won't have enough time to finish.
  3. She'll probably send the report this evening.
  4. As soon as the lecture ends, we'll leave.
  5. Will you be able to attend tomorrow?
These are text-based listening drills, not recorded audio. For realistic listening practice, read the lines aloud, use a text-to-speech tool, or have a partner read them without showing you the text.

14.3 What to listen for

  • Contracted forms such as I'll, she'll and they'll.
  • The negative contraction won't.
  • The base form following will.
  • Time expressions that establish the future reference.
  • Conditional words such as if and unless.
  • Whether the speaker is predicting, offering or making a promise.

Before moving to another grammar topic, check that you can do the following.

  • Explain the main uses of will in your own words.

  • Form negative statements with will not and won't.

  • Recognise common contractions in listening.
  • Use will for predictions, spontaneous decisions, promises and offers.
