Beginner level

Sentence structure

A complete sentence has a subject, a verb and a finished idea. Everything else is detail.

In plain words

Think of a sentence as a small machine with two essential parts: someone or something (the subject) and what they do or are (the verb). Add a finished thought and you have a sentence. Most learner errors are not about difficult grammar. They are about a missing part, a part in the wrong place, or two machines joined together with nothing to connect them. Before you ask whether a sentence sounds good, check that the three basics are there.

How to build it

Basic Subject + verb: Birds fly.

Object Subject + verb + object: She reads books.

With be Subject + be + description: He is a doctor. The test was difficult.

Extras Add place and time: I studied grammar in the library yesterday.

When we use it

The minimum: subject + verb

Rain fell. Prices rose.

Some verbs need nothing after them.

Subject + verb + object

The government built new schools.

Some verbs need an object: you cannot just “build”.

Subject + be + description

The results were surprising.

Be links the subject to a description, a job or a state.

Questions

Where do you live? Does she work here?

The helper verb comes before the subject. With no other helper, add do, does or did.

Where the extras go

I usually study at home in the evening.

Usual order: manner, place, time. Frequency words go before the main verb, but after be.

Look alike, but not the same

Because I was tired.
I stayed home because I was tired.

The first is a fragment: it starts a reason but never gives the main idea.

It is important, we must act.
It is important, so we must act.

Two full sentences need a joiner, not just a comma.

Is raining.
It is raining.

English needs a subject, even when it is only “it”.

A sentence can be long and still be simple. What matters is that it has a main subject and verb that make complete sense on their own.

Mistakes people make

Because the weather was bad. → We cancelled the trip because the weather was bad.

Attach the because-part to a main clause.

Is very important to sleep well. → It is very important to sleep well.

Do not drop the subject.

She very likes music. → She likes music very much.

Very does not go before a verb.

I like very much music. → I like music very much.

Do not put an adverb between a verb and its object.

Always I go early. → I always go early.

Frequency adverbs go before the main verb.

Why you are late? → Why are you late?

In questions the helper verb comes before the subject.

Going deeper

Core test: subject + verb + finished idea

Cover everything except the first clause. Ask: who or what? did what? Does it make sense alone? If it starts with because, although, which, when, if or while and there is nothing else, it is a fragment. Fragments are very common in Task 2 introductions.

The five basic patterns

Almost every sentence is built on one of these: S+V (Prices fell), S+V+O (She wrote an essay), S+V+C (He became a teacher), S+V+O+O (He gave me a book), S+V+O+C (They made him captain). Add extras freely to any of them.

Clauses and phrases

A clause has its own subject and verb. A phrase does not. “After the lecture” is a phrase. “After the lecture ended” is a clause. Only a main clause can stand alone.

Where time and place go

Time words can open or close a sentence: Yesterday I called her. I called her yesterday. Do not squeeze them between verb and object. When you have both, place comes before time: She studied in London in 2019.

Never drop the subject or the object

English almost never drops the subject: say “It is raining” and “There is a problem”. Keep the object when the verb needs one: “I like it”, not “I like”.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • Find the verb first. Underline it, then ask who or what does it.
  • Read the first clause alone. If it cannot stand by itself, it is not a sentence.
  • One idea, one main verb. Two main verbs with no joiner means a run-on.
  • Check the first word. Because, Although, Which or When at the start is a warning sign.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “Which” sentences: “Which is a serious problem.” is a fragment unless it follows its noun in the same sentence.
  • Very long sentences: Count the main verbs. Each one needs its own subject and a joiner.
  • Lists: Keep items in the same form: “reading, writing and speaking”, not “reading, to write and speaking”.

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Check every sentence has a subject and a main verb.
  • Put frequency adverbs before the main verb.
  • Say “It is…” or “There is…” when you have no other subject.
  • Keep long sentences to two or three ideas.

Don’t

  • Don’t write a because-clause alone as a full sentence.
  • Don’t put an adverb between verb and object.
  • Don’t join two sentences with only a comma.
  • Don’t drop the subject: “Is important”.

Why we get it wrong

Mother-tongue transfer

Some languages allow the subject to be dropped or put the verb last. Learners carry that order into English.

Try this: Say the idea in the pattern S + V + O and see if it still fits.

Writing as you think

Under time pressure ideas arrive in pieces and get written as pieces.

Try this: Finish each sentence, then pause for one second before the next.

Copying spoken English

Speech is full of fragments. Writing needs the full sentence.

Try this: Reread every sentence and ask: could it stand alone?

Small habits that help

  • Underline the subject and verb of every sentence in your last essay.
  • Turn five fragments from emails or posts into full sentences.
  • Read your writing aloud and listen for sentences that stop too soon.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: answer in full sentences. “I live in a small town” beats “small town”.
  • Writing: mix short and long sentences. Every one needs a subject and a finite verb.
  • Reading: in a long sentence, find the subject and main verb first. The rest is detail.
  • Listening: expect the order S + V + O, with the extras at the end.

Real world: in emails, short complete sentences sound clear and professional.

Examiners reward accuracy as well as range, so a correct simple sentence always beats a broken complex one.

Practice

Spot the mistake

  1. Because I was ill.
    Show answerI stayed at home because I was ill.: attach the reason to a main clause.
  2. Is a big problem.
    Show answerIt is a big problem.: add the subject.
  3. I like very much football.
    Show answerI like football very much.: keep verb and object together.
  4. Always she arrives late.
    Show answerShe always arrives late.: frequency adverb before the main verb.

Say it another way

  1. Fix the fragment: “Although it was expensive.”
    Show answerAlthough it was expensive, I bought it.
  2. Put in order: yesterday / I / in the park / met / a friend
    Show answerI met a friend in the park yesterday.

Fill the gaps

(1) ___ is cold today. I (2) ___ wear a coat. Why (3) ___ you leaving? (4) ___ are two buses.

Show answers1 It · 2 usually / always · 3 are · 4 There

Speak and write

  • Describe your home in five full sentences. Check each has a subject and a verb.
  • Write one sentence in each of the five patterns about your own life.

Making it stick

  • Write five sentences about your day. Underline the subject and verb in each.
  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Redo the quiz after a day, three days and a week.
  • Read examples aloud, then write three about your own life.
