Speaking practice

Ideas, tips, mistakes to avoid, 50 practice prompts with selfie recording, and tongue twisters.

Ideas, tips and exercises

What to do
  • Speak daily for 10 minutes. Short and regular beats long and rare.
  • Answer with a reason and an example. “I like trains. They are cheap, and last Eid I travelled home for only 300 taka.”
  • Use linking words: because, however, for example, as a result.
  • Shadowing: play a short clip, then repeat it at the same speed and rhythm.
  • Record, listen, fix one thing each time (a sound, a tense, or a filler word).
What not to do
  • Don't memorise long answers. Examiners notice, and your score drops.
  • Don't stay silent to find the perfect word. Say “What I mean is…” and explain it simply.
  • Don't speak too fast. Clear and steady is better.
  • Don't answer with one word. “Yes.” becomes “Yes, because I find it relaxing.”
Where we make mistakes (with examples)
  • Tense: ✗ “Yesterday I go to market.” ✓ “Yesterday I went to the market.”
  • Subject–verb agreement: ✗ “People is happy.” ✓ “People are happy.”
  • Articles: ✗ “She is engineer.” ✓ “She is an engineer.”
  • Gerund / infinitive: ✗ “I enjoy to read.” ✓ “I enjoy reading.”
  • Word stress: pho-TO-gra-phy, not PHO-to-gra-phy.
  • Fillers: too many “um, like, you know”. Pause silently instead.
  • Sounds: /θ/ in “think” is not /t/; /v/ in “very” is not /b/ or /w/.

Detailed speaking guide

1. How the IELTS Speaking test works (11–14 minutes)

Part 1 (4–5 min): short questions about home, study, work, hobbies. Part 2 (3–4 min): a cue card; you get 1 minute to prepare and speak for 1–2 minutes. Part 3 (4–5 min): deeper, abstract questions linked to Part 2.

2. The 4 scoring criteria and what to do
  • Fluency and coherence: keep talking, even slowly. Use linkers: “firstly”, “on top of that”, “as a result”. Say “Let me think” instead of staying silent.
  • Vocabulary: use topic words and a few precise ones. “It was very good” → “It was remarkably enjoyable.”
  • Grammar: mix simple and complex sentences. “I like it because I can relax, which helps after a long day.”
  • Pronunciation: clear word stress, rhythm and intonation matter more than an accent.
3. Part 1: the Answer–Reason–Example method

Question: Do you like cooking?
✗ “Yes.”
✓ “Yes, I do. It helps me relax after studying. For example, on Fridays I usually cook rice and chicken curry for my family.”
Aim for 2–3 sentences (15–30 words).

4. Part 2: the cue card structure

Cue card: Describe a place you visited. Use your 1 minute to write 5 keywords, not sentences. Then speak in this order: What and where → when and who with → what you did → why it was special. If you finish early, add a feeling or a result. Tip: use past tenses first, then switch to present for your opinion (“Looking back, I think…”).

5. Part 3: the PEEL method

Point → Explain → Example → Link back.
Question: Why do people travel?
“Mainly to relax (point). Daily life is stressful, so a change of scene helps people recharge (explain). For instance, my uncle always visits the hills after busy months at work (example). That is why travel is so popular (link).”

6. Pronunciation: stress, linking and intonation
  • Word stress: pho-TO-gra-phy, de-TE-ri-o-rate, ‘ENG-lish’.
  • Linking: “turn off” sounds like “tur-noff”; “an apple” like “a-napple”.
  • Intonation: go down at the end of statements, up for yes/no questions. Avoid a flat voice.
  • Common Bangladeshi-speaker errors: “v” said as “b”, /θ/ said as “t”, and the vowel in “bed” and “bad” sounding the same. Practise with the tongue twisters below.
7. Natural fillers you may use (a few, not many)

“Well,” “Let me see,” “That is a good question,” “To be honest,” “What I mean is…”. Avoid long “ummm…” and “you know” again and again.

8. A 7-day practice plan (20 minutes a day)
  • Day 1: read two model scripts aloud and copy the rhythm.
  • Day 2: Part 1, answer 10 questions with Answer–Reason–Example.
  • Day 3: Part 2, speak for 2 minutes and record yourself.
  • Day 4: Part 3, practise PEEL on 5 questions.
  • Day 5: 10 tongue twisters, slow then fast.
  • Day 6: repeat a Part 2 topic and compare with Day 3.
  • Day 7: a full mock test with a friend or the selfie camera.

Model scripts to learn from

Read each aloud, then use the same shape with your own ideas. Do not memorise them word for word.

Your hometown

I come from Sylhet, a green city surrounded by tea gardens. It is quite small, so everyone knows each other, and that gives it a warm feeling. What I love most is the weather. Honestly, nothing beats a cloudy morning there with a cup of tea. However, it can be crowded during festivals. All in all, it is a place I will always call home.

Why it works: Opening fact, a feeling, a reason, a contrast (“however”), and a closing line.

A person you admire

The person I admire most is my mother. She works as a teacher, and she has taught me to stay patient. For example, when I failed an exam last year, she did not shout. Instead, she sat with me and helped me plan again. That is why I look up to her.

Why it works: Name, job, a short story as an example, then “That is why…”.

Your favourite food

My favourite food is biryani. It is a rice dish with meat and spices, and it smells amazing. We usually make it on special occasions, so it reminds me of family time. To be honest, I could eat it every day, although it is not very healthy.

Why it works: Describe, link to a memory, give an honest opinion.

A memorable journey

Last winter I travelled to Cox's Bazar by bus. It took about ten hours, and I was exhausted. But when I saw the sea at sunrise, I forgot everything. What made it special was that I went with my closest friends. If I had the chance, I would go again tomorrow.

Why it works: Past tense for the story, then a past/present link and a second conditional to finish.

Learning English

I started learning English at school, but I only improved when I began speaking daily. At first I felt shy, so I recorded myself. Listening back helped me notice my mistakes. These days I watch videos and repeat sentences, which has made me more confident.

Why it works: Present perfect (“has made”), a clear routine, and a result.
