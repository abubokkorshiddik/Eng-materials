1. The core idea of the past simple

The past simple describes an action, event, or state viewed as belonging to a finished time in the past.

The most important question Am I describing something that happened or was true in a past period that I am treating as finished? If yes, the past simple may be appropriate.
Example 1

A completed event

I completed my assignment yesterday.

Why? The assignment was completed, and yesterday is a finished past time.

Example 2

A past state

My family lived in Rangpur when I was a child.

Why? The sentence describes a situation during a past period of life.

Example 3

A historical event

The researchers published the results in 2022.

Why? The publication is located in a particular, finished year.

Example 4

A past routine

We walked to school every morning.

Why? The sentence describes a repeated habit during a past period.

Does past simple always mean that something happened only once?

No. It can describe one action, repeated actions, or a situation that continued for years.

One event: I visited Dhaka last Friday.

Repeated action: I visited my grandparents every summer.

Long past state: She worked at the hospital for ten years.

Why this matters Do not choose a tense only because the action was short or long. Choose it because of how you present the event and its relationship to the time being discussed. A ten-year period can be expressed with the past simple when that period is finished.

2. Past simple: sentence structure

There are two important patterns: sentences with an ordinary action verb, such as work or go, and sentences with the verb be, which becomes was or were.

A. Ordinary verbs: positive statements

Subject + past form of the main verb + other information.
SubjectPast verbExample
IworkedI worked yesterday.
ShestudiedShe studied statistics.
TheywentThey went to the library.
WecompletedWe completed the project last month.
Why is the verb in the past form? In a positive past-simple sentence with an ordinary verb, the main verb normally carries the past marking: work → worked, go → went. Unlike the present simple, the form does not change between I, you, he, she, we, and they. We say both “I worked” and “she worked,” not “she works” when the intended meaning is past simple.

B. Ordinary verbs: negative statements

Subject + did not (didn't) + base form of the verb.

Correct: I did not attend the meeting.

Correct: She didn't understand the question.

Incorrect: She didn't understood the question.

Why is “understood” wrong after “didn't”? The auxiliary did already carries the past tense. The main verb returns to its base form: didn't understand, not didn't understood.

C. Ordinary verbs: questions

Did + subject + base form of the verb + ...?
QuestionShort answerReason
Did you study yesterday?Yes, I did. / No, I didn't.Did marks the past tense.
Did she call you?Yes, she did. / No, she didn't.Use the base verb after did.
Where did they go?They went home.The question word comes before did.
Why did he leave?He felt unwell.Did + subject + base verb.
Never put two past markers together in the same ordinary verb phrase

Incorrect: Did she went?

Correct: Did she go?

Incorrect: They didn't completed it.

Correct: They didn't complete it.

D. The verb “be”: was and were

The verb be is different. Its past forms are was and were. In ordinary past-simple statements with be, do not add did.

SubjectPositiveNegativeQuestion
II was tired.I was not tired.Was I tired?
He / She / ItShe was ready.She wasn't ready.Was she ready?
You / We / TheyThey were late.They weren't late.Were they late?
Remember: I/he/she/it → was. You/we/they → were. This is the usual pattern for statements, but you takes were even when referring to one person.

With “be”

She was busy.

Was she busy?

Did she be busy?

Be forms questions by moving was/were before the subject.

With an ordinary verb

She worked yesterday.

Did she work yesterday?

Worked she yesterday?

Ordinary verbs generally use did in questions and negatives.

E. Negative questions and question tags

Negative questions often express surprise, check an assumption, or invite agreement.

Didn't you receive my email? — I thought you had received it.

Wasn't the lecture interesting? — The speaker expected it to be interesting.

You finished the report, didn't you? — A positive statement followed by a negative tag.

She wasn't at home, was she? — A negative statement followed by a positive tag.

3. Regular and irregular verbs

Regular verbs normally form the past simple by adding -ed or -d. Irregular verbs have other forms that you must learn.

A. Regular spelling rules

RuleBase formPast simpleExample
Usually add -edworkworkedI worked yesterday.
Already ends in e: add -dlivelivedWe lived there.
Consonant + y: change y to i and add -edstudystudiedShe studied English.
Vowel + y: keep y and add -edplayplayedThey played football.
Some short stressed verbs double the final consonantstopstoppedThe bus stopped.
Final c commonly takes k before -edpanicpanickedHe panicked.
Why is “studied” correct but “played” also correct? In study, y follows a consonant, so it changes to i before -ed. In play, y follows a vowel, so it stays: played.

Consonant doubling depends on spelling patterns and stress. Compare stop → stopped with open → opened. Do not double every final consonant.

Important: past simple is not the past participle

Use “went” in “I went yesterday,” but “gone” in “I have gone home.”

4. Pronouncing regular -ed endings

The spelling ending -ed has three common pronunciations. It does not always create an extra syllable.

/t/

Worked, watched, laughed

After many voiceless sounds, such as /k/, /ʃ/, and /f/.

“Worked” normally has one syllable, not two.

/d/

Played, cleaned, opened

After most voiced sounds and vowel sounds.

“Played” normally has one syllable.

/ɪd/ or /əd/

Wanted, needed, decided

After the sounds /t/ and /d/.

This ending adds a syllable: want-ed, need-ed.

IELTS Listening and Speaking tip: Practise hearing the difference between “worked” and “wanted.” Do not pronounce every -ed ending as a separate syllable. Clear pronunciation matters more than imitating a particular accent.

5. The main uses of the past simple

Use 1: A completed action at a finished past time

We collected the data in 2023.

I submitted my application last week.

The conference ended two hours ago.

Why? The actions are presented as completed within a past time frame.

Why not present perfect? In standard usage, we normally do not use the present perfect with a specific, finished time such as yesterday or in 2023 when that time is part of the same sentence.

Compare: “I submitted it yesterday” and “I have submitted it.” The second focuses on the present relevance and does not specify a finished time.

Use 2: A sequence of completed events

She opened the dataset, checked the missing values, and saved the cleaned file.

He woke up, ate breakfast, and left for work.

Why? The past simple presents events in sequence. This is useful for telling stories and explaining completed procedures.

Do not do this: Do not change tense randomly between steps if the events belong to the same finished sequence. Change tense only when the meaning or time reference changes.

Use 3: Past habits and repeated actions

When I was at school, I walked to class every day.

My family visited our village during every holiday.

Why? The action happened repeatedly during a past period. The past simple does not tell us that the action happened only once.

Compare with “used to”: “I walked to school” reports a past habit. “I used to walk to school” more explicitly highlights a former habit or situation that is no longer true or is no longer regular.

Use 4: Past states and situations

The village had a small hospital.

She knew the answer.

They lived near the river for several years.

Why? Past simple is not limited to physical actions. It also describes states, knowledge, possession, and living situations in the past.

Important: Some verbs describing states, such as know, believe, and own, are not normally used in continuous forms when they have their usual stative meaning. Say “I knew the answer,” not usually “I was knowing the answer.”

Use 5: Historical facts and past events

The first survey took place in 1995.

The government introduced the policy in 2018.

The researchers published their findings in 2024.

Why? These statements locate events in finished historical periods. The past simple is common in history books, reports, and academic writing about completed research.

Use 6: Past conditions and results

The road flooded after heavy rainfall.

Many residents moved to safer areas.

The temperature dropped overnight.

Why? The tense presents events and results as part of a completed past episode.

Use 7: Finished periods of work or study

I studied statistics at university.

She worked on the project from June to September.

Why? The period is presented as finished. If the activity is still continuing and you want to emphasize its connection with the present, another tense may be more appropriate.

Use 8: Narratives, biographies, and anecdotes

At first, I felt nervous. Then the examiner asked a simple question, and I became more confident.

Why? The past simple is the main tense for the events that move a past narrative forward. Other tenses, such as the past continuous, can provide background information.

6. Negatives, questions, and short answers in detail

A. Negative forms

PositiveNegativeExplanation
I attended.I did not attend.Did + base verb.
She went.She did not go.Went changes back to go.
They understood.They didn't understand.Understood changes back to understand.
He was ready.He was not ready.Was remains the main verb.
We were present.We were not present.Were remains the main verb.

B. Yes/no questions

Did + subject + base verb?

Did you finish? — Yes, I did. / No, I didn't.

Did she call? — Yes, she did. / No, she didn't.

Was he ill? — Yes, he was. / No, he wasn't.

C. Wh- questions

Question word + did + subject + base verb?

What did you study? I studied statistics.

Where did they go? They went to the university.

Why did she leave? She felt unwell.

When did the event happen? It happened in May.

D. The important exception: questions about the subject

When who or what is the subject of the question, we usually do not use did.

QuestionMeaningStructure
Who called you?Who performed the action?Who + past verb.
Who did you call?Whom did you contact?Who + did + subject + base verb.
What happened?What event occurred?What + past verb.
What did you see?What did you observe?What + did + subject + base verb.
Why is the structure different? In “Who called you?”, who is the subject, so the past form called carries the tense. In “Who did you call?”, you is the subject and who is the object, so the question uses did + call.

E. Questions with “be”

Was / Were + subject + ...?

Correct: “Where were you yesterday?”

Incorrect: “Where did you be yesterday?”

Correct: “Why was the road closed?”

F. Polite requests and indirect questions

In an indirect question, use statement word order after the opening phrase.

Direct: Where did she go?

Indirect: Could you tell me where she went?

Incorrect: Could you tell me where did she go?

This distinction is especially useful in formal speaking and polite communication.

7. Time expressions: clues, not automatic rules

ExpressionExampleWhy it fits
YesterdayI called yesterday.A finished day.
Last week/month/yearWe travelled last year.A finished time period.
... agoShe arrived two hours ago.Counts back from now to a past event.
In + past yearThe policy changed in 2019.A specified historical period.
When + past clauseWhen I was young, I played outside.A past time or period.
Then / at that timeAt that time, the area had fewer roads.A past reference point.
From ... to ...She worked there from 2020 to 2023.The period is finished.

Does “for” always mean present perfect?

No. The meaning of the time period matters.

Finished period

She worked there for five years.

The work period is presented as finished.

Continuing period

She has worked there for five years.

This normally suggests she still works there now.

Does “today” always require the present perfect?

No. Consider the speaker's time reference and intended meaning.

I spoke to her earlier today. A completed conversation at a past time.

I have spoken to her twice today. The speaker views today's period as still relevant or ongoing.

Usage can vary by context and dialect. The most important point is to understand whether you are presenting a finished past event or connecting it to the present.

8. Past simple versus other tenses

A. Past simple vs present simple

Past simplePresent simpleDifference
I worked in a hospital in 2022.I work in a hospital.Past finished period vs current situation.
She visited us every week as a child.She visits us every week.Former habit vs present habit.
The population increased in 2010.The population increases every year.Completed past change vs general or repeated pattern.

B. Past simple vs present continuous

Past simple: I studied last night.

Present continuous: I am studying now.

Why? The first refers to a completed past activity; the second describes an activity in progress now.

C. Past simple vs past continuous

Past simplePast continuousMeaning
I cooked dinner.I was cooking dinner.Completed event vs activity in progress at a past reference point.
It started to rain.I was walking home.The past simple can identify the event that interrupts a background activity.
When I arrived, she left.When I arrived, she was leaving.Different event relationships and viewpoints.

Common pattern: “I was studying when the lights went out.” The longer background action is in the past continuous; the event that occurred is in the past simple. This is a useful pattern, not an absolute rule for every sentence.

D. Past simple vs present perfect

Past simplePresent perfectWhy the choice changes
I visited Cox's Bazar in 2023.I have visited Cox's Bazar.Specific finished time vs experience up to now.
She lost her keys yesterday.She has lost her keys.Past event at a specified time vs present result or relevance.
We finished the report last night.We have finished the report.Finished past time vs current completion.
He lived there for two years.He has lived there for two years.Normally a finished period vs a period continuing to now.
Common IELTS error: “I have completed my bachelor's degree in 2026.” When the sentence explicitly places completion in a finished time, the usual choice is “I completed my bachelor's degree in 2026.” If you omit the finished time and focus on the present fact, “I have completed my bachelor's degree” may be appropriate.

E. Past simple vs past perfect

Past simple: When I arrived, the lecture started.

Past perfect: When I arrived, the lecture had started.

The first sentence can suggest that the lecture started at or after the arrival, depending on context. The second clearly states that the lecture had already started before the arrival.

Use the past perfect when you need to make the earlier of two past events clear. You do not need to use it for every earlier event if the sequence is already clear.

F. Past simple vs “used to” and “would”

FormExampleUse
Past simpleWe played outside every evening.Reports a past habit.
Used to + base verbWe used to play outside every evening.Highlights a former habit or state that no longer applies or is no longer usual.
Would + base verbAfter dinner, Grandpa would tell us stories.Repeated past actions, especially in stories; not normally used for past states.

Why not “I would know him” for a former state? The habitual past meaning of would does not normally express a state such as knowing or owning. “I used to know him” is suitable.

G. Past simple vs past perfect continuous

Past simple: She worked for three hours.

Past perfect continuous: She had been working for three hours when the power failed.

The first presents the work as a completed past period. The second emphasizes the duration of an activity up to a later point in the past.

9. Advanced uses and tricky cases

A. Stative verbs

Stative verbs describe states rather than actions. Common examples include know, believe, understand, own, belong, prefer, and seem.

I understood the instructions.

I was understanding the instructions.

She owned a small shop.

She was owning a small shop.

These are usual patterns for the meanings shown. Some verbs can also have dynamic meanings and can then appear in continuous forms: “I was thinking about the problem” describes an activity of thinking.

B. “When” and “while”

When can introduce a point, event, or period in the past. While often introduces an activity in progress.

When I arrived, the meeting began.

When I arrived, the meeting was taking place.

While I was studying, my phone rang.

Do not assume that when always takes past simple or while always takes past continuous. Select the tense based on the meaning of the clause.

C. Conditional sentences

The past simple can express an unreal or hypothetical present or future situation in the second conditional.

If I had more time, I would read every day.

If I were you, I would practise speaking aloud.

Here, had and were do not refer to a finished past event. They express distance from reality. This is a different use from the ordinary past-time meaning.

“If I were you” is the conventional form in formal advice. “If I was you” is also heard in informal usage.

D. Polite or tentative language

I wanted to ask you a question.

I wondered whether you could help me.

These past forms can make a request sound less direct. They do not necessarily mean that the wanting or wondering happened only in the past.

E. Reported speech

Direct speech: She said, “I feel tired.”

Reported speech: She said that she felt tired.

When the reporting verb is in the past, a present form often shifts back in reported speech. This is common, but not compulsory in every context; the original tense may be retained when the statement is still true or the speaker wants to emphasize its current validity.

F. Negative meaning without “didn't”

Words such as never can make a past-simple statement negative in meaning.

I never missed a class.

She never visited that place.

Do not normally combine never with another negative in standard English: “I didn't never go” is not the standard form for this meaning. Say “I never went” or “I didn't ever go.”

Five habits that improve your accuracy

  1. Find the time reference. Ask whether the sentence is about a finished past period, the present, or a hypothetical situation.
  2. Check for did or didn't. If it is followed by an ordinary main verb, use the base form.
  3. Check was and were. Do not use did with the ordinary past-simple form of be.
  4. Check irregular verbs. Learn the past form, especially frequent verbs such as go, make, take, see, and write.
  5. Check the whole meaning. A sentence can be grammatically formed but still use the wrong tense for the intended time relationship.

11. Past simple for IELTS Writing

A. IELTS Academic Writing Task 1: past data

Use the past simple when describing data for a period that has finished. If a chart shows values for 2000–2020, past simple is often appropriate for the changes during those years.

Example: The number of visitors increased from 20,000 in 2010 to 35,000 in 2015.

Example: The proportion fell slightly between 2005 and 2010.

Example: In 2018, rural expenditure exceeded urban expenditure.

Why? These sentences report changes and comparisons in completed periods.

B. Choose the tense from the chart's time period

Chart informationTypical tense choiceExample
Data for 2000–2020Past simpleThe rate increased between 2000 and 2020.
Current figuresPresent simpleThe chart shows three categories.
Predictions for 2040Future or projection languageThe figure is projected to rise by 2040.
Past trend with a present resultChoose based on the claimThe rate rose in the past and remains high today.
Do not do this: Do not describe every chart in the past tense simply because it contains numbers. A chart may show current facts, future projections, or a mixture of historical and projected data. Match each statement to its actual time period.

C. Academic reporting and research descriptions

Past simple

The researchers collected data from 64 districts.

The analysis identified substantial regional variation.

Use for completed methods, analyses, and findings.

Present simple

Table 2 shows the regional variation.

The results suggest a possible association.

Use when describing what a table shows now or presenting the current interpretation.

D. Avoid repetitive and weak reporting

Instead of repeating “increased” in every sentence, use accurate alternatives when they fit the data.

rose · grew · climbed · increased · declined · fell · dropped · decreased · remained stable · fluctuated

These are vocabulary choices, not different tenses. The verb still needs the correct past form: rose, grew, fell, remained, and fluctuated.

E. A model Task 1 paragraph

The proportion of households with internet access increased from 35% in 2010 to 62% in 2020. Growth was particularly rapid between 2015 and 2020, when the figure rose by 17 percentage points. By contrast, the proportion of households without access declined throughout the period.

This is an illustrative example, not data from a real chart. Notice the finished years and the past forms: increased, was, rose, and declined.

F. IELTS Writing Task 2

Task 2 often discusses present-day issues, so do not use past simple throughout the essay. Use it for historical evidence, personal examples, or completed past events, then choose other tenses for current arguments and general truths.

Historical example: In the past, many rural communities relied on traditional methods of communication.

Current claim: Digital communication now plays a central role in everyday life.

Past evidence: A local programme introduced free health checks in 2021.

G. What IELTS examiners need from your grammar

  • Use the tense that matches the time and meaning.
  • Keep verb forms accurate, including irregular past forms.
  • Use a range of structures naturally rather than forcing complicated grammar into every sentence.
  • Proofread auxiliary verbs, verb endings, and shifts between past and present.
  • Remember that grammar is only one part of the writing assessment; task response, coherence, and vocabulary also matter.

12. Using the past simple in all four IELTS skills

Speaking: Part 1

Question: Did you enjoy school?

Answer: Yes, I did. I particularly enjoyed science lessons because they encouraged me to ask questions.

The question asks about a past experience, so past simple is appropriate.

Speaking: Part 2

Topic: Describe a memorable event.

Answer opening: Last year, I attended a research seminar that changed the way I thought about public health.

Past simple helps establish the event and move the story forward.

Speaking: Part 3

Question: How did education change in your country?

Answer: Access to online learning expanded considerably during the pandemic.

Past simple describes a change during a completed period. Present simple may be needed for the current situation.

Writing Task 1

Example: The percentage rose steadily from 25% in 2000 to 45% in 2010.

The figures refer to a completed historical period.

Writing Task 2

Example: Several cities introduced restrictions to reduce traffic congestion.

The sentence reports a completed past policy action. Discuss its present effects using the tense that fits the claim.

Reading

Watch for dates, historical sequences, and changes in verb form. “The team developed a model in 2019” reports a past event; “the model predicts future risk” describes its function.

Tense helps you understand the order and time reference of information.

Listening

Listen for forms such as worked, went, found, took, said, and time clues such as last week or three years ago.

Recognising irregular verbs and reduced pronunciation helps you identify who did what and when.

Listening: distractors

A speaker might say, “We planned to meet on Tuesday, but we actually met on Thursday.”

Listen for corrections and final information. Do not select an answer just because you heard a matching word.

Useful past-simple speaking patterns

Giving a reason: I chose that subject because I enjoyed working with data.

Describing a challenge: At first, I found the task difficult, but I gradually became more confident.

Describing a result: The project helped me understand how statistical methods could support public health decisions.

Comparing past and present: I used to study alone, but now I often practise with other learners.

13. What to do and what to avoid

Do this

  • Identify when the event happened.
  • Use the past form in positive statements.
  • Use did/didn't + base verb for ordinary verbs.
  • Use was/were correctly with be.
  • Learn irregular verbs in sentences.
  • Use past simple for finished historical data.
  • Change tense when the time reference changes.
  • Read your sentences aloud to check naturalness.

Avoid this

  • Do not use did + a past-form main verb.
  • Do not use present perfect with a specific finished time in the usual standard pattern.
  • Do not assume all verbs take -ed.
  • Do not use did with ordinary past forms of be.
  • Do not switch tenses without a reason.
  • Do not force past simple into present-day descriptions.
  • Do not memorise rules without writing your own examples.
  • Do not use complex grammar if a simple accurate sentence communicates the idea better.

A simple decision process

  1. Find the time: Is the sentence about a past event or a past period?
  2. Check the meaning: Is the event completed, a repeated past habit, a past state, or part of a narrative?
  3. Build the sentence: Use the past verb in a positive sentence, or did/didn't + base verb for ordinary verbs.
  4. Check “be”: Use was/were without did.
  5. Check the context: Does another tense communicate the intended time relationship more accurately?
  6. Proofread: Look for irregular verbs, spelling, and auxiliary mistakes.

Question: Describe a time when you learned something useful.

Try to include:

  • When and where it happened.
  • What you learned.
  • What you did to learn it.
  • Why the experience was useful.

Use the model for structure, not as a response to memorise. Personal, specific details help make your own answer natural.

16. Revision checklist
