Punctuation

Punctuation shows where an idea ends and how the parts of a sentence connect.

In plain words

Punctuation is traffic control for your reader. A full stop means stop. A comma means slow down. A semicolon links two closely related complete ideas. The single biggest punctuation mistake in IELTS is the comma splice: joining two full sentences with only a comma. If you can fix that, you have fixed most of your punctuation.

How to build it

Full stop ends a complete idea: The test was hard.

Comma separates parts: lists, openings, extra information, and a joiner between two full sentences.

Semicolon joins two related full sentences: It was late; we went home.

Colon introduces a list or explanation: I need three things: time, money and focus.

Apostrophe belonging or missing letters: Ali’s book, don’t.

Hyphen joins words: well-known, long-term.

When we use it

Full stop

Every complete sentence ends with one.

Use ? for questions. Exclamation marks are rare in academic writing.

Commas in lists

I bought apples, oranges and bananas.

A comma before and is optional, but be consistent.

Comma after an opening

However, prices rose. Although it rained, we went out.

Opening words and clauses get a comma.

Commas for extra information

My brother, who lives in Dubai, is a doctor.

A comma on both sides.

Comma + joiner

It rained, so we stayed home.

Use a comma before and, but, so or or when they join two full sentences.

Semicolon and colon

Some prefer cities; others prefer villages.

Semicolon: two complete ideas. Colon: a list or explanation after a complete sentence.

Small marks, big differences

It’s raining.
Its colour is red.

it’s = it is. its = belonging to it.

The student’s books
The students’ books

One owner vs several owners.

It rained; however, we went out.
It rained, however we went out.

However needs a semicolon or full stop before it, and a comma after.

I need three things: time, money and focus.
I need time, money and focus.

Use a colon only after a complete sentence.

If you are not sure about a comma, ask yourself whether you are joining two complete sentences. If so, a comma alone is not enough.

Mistakes people make

It was late, we went home. → It was late, so we went home.

Comma splice.

I like tea, I don’t like coffee. → I like tea, but I don’t like coffee.

Comma splice.

However it was late, we stayed. → It was late; however, we stayed.

However is not a joiner.

The students book is old. → The student’s book is old.

An apostrophe shows the owner.

Its raining. → It’s raining.

It’s means it is.

I dont know. → I don’t know.

Contractions need an apostrophe.

There are three reasons, money, time and health. → There are three reasons: money, time and health.

Use a colon to introduce the list.

The CD’s are cheap. → The CDs are cheap.

No apostrophe in plurals.

The man, who called, is my uncle (identifying). → The man who called is my uncle.

No commas for essential information.

Going deeper

Core test: two full sentences?

Split the sentence at the comma. Can both halves stand alone? If so, you have a comma splice.

Four ways to fix a comma splice

1 Full stop: It was late. We went home. 2 Semicolon: It was late; we went home. 3 Comma + joiner: It was late, so we went home. 4 Supporting clause: Because it was late, we went home.

However, therefore, moreover

These are adverbs, not joiners. Use a full stop or semicolon before them and a comma after: It rained; however, we went out.

Commas and meaning

My brother, who lives in Dubai, is a doctor (I have one brother). My brother who lives in Dubai is a doctor (I have more than one). Commas change meaning.

Apostrophes

Contractions: don’t, it’s. Singular owner: the boy’s. Plural owner: the boys’. Irregular plural: the children’s. No apostrophe in plain plurals or dates: CDs, 1990s.

Capital letters

Sentence starts, names, days, months, languages and nationalities. Not seasons or school subjects (unless a language).

Numbers and quotes

Spell out numbers under ten in formal writing. In Task 1 use numerals for data. Use quotation marks for direct speech.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • Two full sentences? Never join with only a comma.
  • However, therefore, moreover. Semicolon or full stop before, comma after.
  • Its or it’s? If you cannot say “it is”, write its.
  • Opening word or phrase? Add a comma: However, In addition, For example,

Where exams try to catch you

  • Comma splices in long Task 2 sentences: Reread every long sentence for hidden full stops.
  • Which clauses: A comma before extra-information which. None before defining that.
  • “Such as” and “including”: No colon after them.
  • Commas between subject and verb: Wrong. Not “The students, are tired.”

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Ask of each comma: am I joining two sentences?
  • Use full stops freely. Short sentences are fine.
  • Use apostrophes only for owners and missing letters.
  • Put a comma after However, For example and In addition.

Don’t

  • Don’t join full sentences with only a comma.
  • Don’t use an apostrophe in plurals.
  • Don’t put a colon after a verb or after “such as”.
  • Don’t put a comma between subject and verb.

Why we get it wrong

Speech rhythm

Commas often follow pauses, but pauses are not grammar.

Try this: Decide by grammar, not by breathing.

Translation

Comma rules differ between languages, and some use commas before every clause.

Try this: Learn the English rules as a separate set.

Long sentences

In long sentences you lose track of where one ends.

Try this: Read each sentence aloud and mark where it ends.

Small habits that help

  • Label every comma in a paragraph: list, opening, extra information or joiner.
  • Hunt for comma splices in your last essay.
  • Learn five joiners: and, but, so, because, although.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: punctuation is not assessed, but clear pauses help.
  • Writing: punctuation counts in Grammatical Range and Accuracy. Comma splices lower the score.
  • Reading: commas show where phrases begin and end, which helps with long sentences.
  • Listening: spell and punctuate carefully, for example apostrophes in names.

Real world: emails and CVs show care when they are punctuated clearly.

Fix comma splices first. They are the most common and most costly punctuation error.

Practice

Spot the mistake

  1. It was late, we went home.
    Show answerIt was late, so we went home.: comma splice.
  2. The students book is old.
    Show answerThe student’s book is old.: the apostrophe shows the owner.
  3. Its a nice day.
    Show answerIt’s a nice day.: it’s means it is.
  4. It was late however we stayed.
    Show answerIt was late; however, we stayed.: However needs the right punctuation.

Say it another way

  1. Fix the splice in two ways: “I was tired, I went to bed.”
    Show answerI was tired, so I went to bed. / I was tired; I went to bed.
  2. Add punctuation: prices rose however people kept buying
    Show answerPrices rose; however, people kept buying.

Making it stick

  • Choose one paragraph a week and check every comma and apostrophe in it.
  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
