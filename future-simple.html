Beginner level

Future simple

Use will for predictions, instant decisions, promises, offers and expectations.

futurepastnowfuture

In plain words

Use will when you decide something right now, when you give an opinion about tomorrow, or when you make a promise. If you already made the plan, English often prefers going to.

How to build it

Positive will + base verb

Negative will not (won't) + base verb

Question Will + subject + base verb?

Time clauses when/before/after/until + present simple

When we use it

Prediction

I think it will rain tonight.

Opinion about the future.

Instant decision

The phone is ringing. I'll answer it.

Decided now.

Promise or offer

I will send the report by Friday.

Commitment.

Expectation (IELTS essays)

Public transport will reduce congestion.

Use may/could when unsure.

Tenses that get mixed up with it

I will study abroad.
I am going to study abroad.

Decision now vs prior plan.

I will meet her.
I am meeting her at 4.

Statement vs arrangement.

The train will leave.
The train leaves at 8.

Prediction vs timetable.

Time words are clues, not rules. The meaning you want decides the tense.

Mistakes people make

She will goes. → She will go.

Base verb after will.

I will don't go. → I won't go.

Not after will.

Call me when I will arrive. → Call me when I arrive.

Present simple in time clause.

She will can drive. → She will be able to drive.

No will + can.

Going deeper

Will, going to, present continuous

Will: instant decision, prediction from opinion, promise. Going to: prior plan or evidence now (“Look at the clouds: it is going to rain”). Present continuous: arranged plan with time/place.

Time clauses and if

After when/before/after/until/as soon as/if, use present simple for future meaning: “If it rains, we will stay home.” Never “if it will rain” for a normal condition. Will in if-clause only for willingness: “If you will help, I will thank you.”

Will + be able to / have to

No “will can/must”. Use will be able to, will have to. Ability: “I will be able to join.”

Formal writing

Predict carefully: may, might, could, is likely to, is expected to. “This will solve the problem” sounds absolute; “This could reduce congestion” is safer and scores better.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • Instant decision. “I'll get it” = will.
  • Plan already made. I'm going to study medicine.
  • Opinion words. think, believe, probably = will.
  • Time-clause shortcut. If the clause starts with when/if and refers to the future, drop will inside it.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “I will meet her tomorrow at 5 (arranged)”: Present continuous is more natural.
  • Won't vs don't: won't = will not.
  • “Will” in if: Don't use: If it will rain.

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Use will for instant decisions, promises, opinion-based predictions.
  • Use going to for existing plans and visible evidence.
  • Use present simple in time/if clauses.
  • Soften predictions with may, could, is likely to.

Don't

  • Don't write will + third-person -s: will goes.
  • Don't write will can/must: use will be able to/have to.
  • Don't use will after when/if/before in future clauses.
  • Don't use will for timetables.

Why we get it wrong

Direct translation

“I will to go”, “I will don't”.

Try this: Pattern: will + base verb, no to, no do.

Overconfidence in essays

Every prediction as will.

Try this: Use hedging for marks and accuracy.

Mixing future forms

will for a plan you already made.

Try this: Plan → going to / present continuous.

Small habits that help

  • Write five promises and five opinions with will.
  • Underline time clauses in your essays and remove any will.
  • In Listening, 'll is a quiet sound; listen for the verb after it.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: plans and predictions: “I think technology will make learning more accessible.”
  • Writing Task 2: predict effects, soften with may/could.
  • Reading: separate prediction from proven fact.
  • Listening: hear 'll and won't.

Real world: promises and offers at work: “I'll email you the file.”

TOEFL, PTE, GRE and bank or civil-service English tests ask the same thing: does the tense match the time you mean?

    Show answerIf I see him, I will tell him.: time clause.

    Show answerIt will probably rain.

Making it stick

  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Before you answer, ask: when did it happen, what do I mean, and what does the verb need?
