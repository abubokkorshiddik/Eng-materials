Intermediate level

Present perfect continuous

Stress the activity and its duration: started in the past, continues or has just stopped.

duration to nowpastnowfuture

In plain words

Same bridge between then and now, but now you are watching the activity itself: how long it has been going on, or what it has left behind, like wet hair after swimming.

How to build it

Positive have/has + been + verb-ing

Negative have/has not been + verb-ing

Question Have/Has + subject + been + verb-ing?

Key words for · since · all day · lately · how long

When we use it

Continuing activity

I have been studying for two hours.

Duration up to now.

Recent activity, visible result

She is tired; she has been running.

The activity explains now.

Repeated effort

They have been trying to fix it all morning.

Focus on the effort.

Temporary process

It has been raining since dawn.

Ongoing process.

Tenses that get mixed up with it

I have written three pages.
I have been writing for three hours.

Result/amount vs duration.

I have read the report.
I have been reading the report.

Finished vs in progress.

She has worked here 10 years.
She has been working here 10 years.

Both possible; continuous stresses activity.

Time words are clues, not rules. The meaning you want decides the tense.

Mistakes people make

I am studying since morning. → I have been studying since morning.

Since → perfect continuous.

She has been study. → She has been studying.

Use -ing after been.

I have been knowing him. → I have known him.

Stative verb.

They has been waiting. → They have been waiting.

have with they.

Going deeper

Core idea: activity with a duration or a visible trace

It describes an activity, started earlier, still going or just stopped. Focus: how long, how much effort, or the visible result (wet hair, tired voice).

Simple vs continuous

“I have read three books” (completed, how many). “I have been reading all day” (activity, how long). With how long / for / since for activities, continuous is natural. With how many/how much, simple is natural.

Stative verbs and short events

Stative: “I have known her for years.” Short events with a duration meaning: “I have been coughing all night” ok; “I have been finding a wallet” not ok.

Typical clues

all day, all morning, lately, recently, these days, for ages, how long. Context decides; don't rely on words alone.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • How long → continuous. for activities.
  • How many → simple. I have written 5 emails.
  • Evidence now. She is wet: she has been swimming.
  • Never with a finished count. Use simple.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “I am working since 8”: Wrong: I have been working since 8.
  • “I have been knowing”: Wrong: stative.
  • “Have you been living here for long?”: Fine; or Have you lived…?

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Use it for duration and visible results.
  • Use how long, for, since.
  • Use simple for completed counts.
  • Check have/has + been.

Don't

  • Don't use am working since.
  • Don't use stative verbs: have been knowing.
  • Don't use it with how many.
  • Don't use finished times with it.

Why we get it wrong

Using present continuous with since

I am studying since.

Try this: Use have been studying.

Confusing result and process

I have been finishing.

Try this: Finish is a result: have finished.

Missing been

I have studying.

Try this: have been studying.

Small habits that help

  • Say what you have been doing lately, in 5 sentences.
  • Notice evidence words: tired, wet, dusty.
  • In Listening, catch been + -ing.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: “I have been practising speaking every evening.”
  • Writing: ongoing processes, used sparingly.
  • Reading: duration phrases signal it.
  • Listening: hear the whole chain have/has + been + -ing.

Real world: explain why you are late or tired.

TOEFL, PTE, GRE and bank or civil-service English tests ask the same thing: does the tense match the time you mean?

Practice

Spot the mistake

  1. I am studying for two hours.
    Show answerI have been studying for two hours.: duration to now.
  2. He has been broke the window.
    Show answerHe has broken the window.: result, not duration.
  3. She has been study.
    Show answerShe has been studying.: -ing.
  4. I have been knowing him.
    Show answerI have known him.: stative.

Say it another way

  1. “I started at 9. It is 11.”
    Show answerI have been working for two hours.
  2. “Wet shoes: rain”
    Show answerMy shoes are wet because it has been raining.

Fill the gaps

I (1) ___ (wait) here for an hour. She (2) ___ (not/call). I (3) ___ (try) her phone all morning, but it is switched off.

Show answers1 have been waiting · 2 hasn't called (or hasn't been calling) · 3 have been trying

Speak and write

  • Explain what you have been doing this week to prepare for your exam.
  • Write five sentences with how long / for / since.

Making it stick

  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Before you answer, ask: when did it happen, what do I mean, and what does the verb need?
  • Redo the quiz after a day, three days and a week.
  • Read examples aloud, then write three about your own life.
