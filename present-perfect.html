Present perfect

Link the past to now: experience, result, or a situation that continues. No finished time.

past → nowpastnowfuture

In plain words

This tense is a bridge between then and now. You are not saying when something happened. You are saying it matters today: the lost keys, the trip you have taken, the flat you have lived in for years.

How to build it

Positive have/has + past participle

Negative have/has not + past participle

Question Have/Has + subject + past participle?

Key words ever · never · just · already · yet · since · for

When we use it

Result now

I have lost my keys.

Past action, present result.

Life experience

She has visited Japan twice.

Time is not stated.

Situation up to now

We have known each other for years.

Began in past, continues.

Unfinished period

I have read three articles this morning.

The morning is not over.

Tenses that get mixed up with it

I saw it yesterday.
I have seen it.

Finished time vs experience.

She lived here for 5 years.
She has lived here for 5 years.

Over vs continuing.

Did you finish?
Have you finished?

Past event vs state now.

Time words are clues, not rules. The meaning you want decides the tense.

Mistakes people make

I have seen him yesterday. → I saw him yesterday.

Finished time: past simple.

She have finished. → She has finished.

has with he/she/it.

I have went there. → I have gone there.

Participle, not past form.

I live here since 2020. → I have lived here since 2020.

Since needs perfect.

Going deeper

1. The one idea behind everything: the time period includes now

Past simple closes a door on the past. Present perfect leaves the door open: the event belongs to a period that still includes the present, or it has a result you can see now.

Open period (present perfect)Closed period (past simple)
today, this week, this year, so far, up to now, in my life, ever, never, recently, latelyyesterday, last week, in 2019, two days ago, when I was young, at 5 p.m.

Ask: “Could this period still add more events?” If yes, perfect. If the period is over, simple past.

2. The three meanings

  1. Result: “I have lost my passport” (I don't have it now).
  2. Experience: “She has climbed Everest” (it is part of her life; no date needed).
  3. Continuing state or unfinished period: “We have lived here since 2018.”

Once you give the exact time (“in 2019”), the sentence usually becomes past simple: “She climbed Everest in 2019.” The perfect introduces a fact; the simple gives the details.

3. For, since, how long

  • for + length: for two years, for a long time.
  • since + starting point: since 2020, since Monday, since I left school.
  • “How long” questions use the perfect for a state that continues: How long have you lived here?
  • Stative verbs (know, live, be, have, own) stay simple: “I have known him for years” (not “have been knowing”).

“Since” clause: past simple inside: “I have been happy since I moved here.”

4. Been vs gone

“She has been to Paris” = she visited and came back. “She has gone to Paris” = she is there now (or on the way). IELTS listening loves this contrast.

5. Just, already, yet, still, ever, never: position

  • just / already / ever / never: between have and the participle: “I have just finished.”
  • yet: end of negatives and questions: “Have you finished yet?” “I haven't finished yet.”
  • already: also at the end for surprise: “You've finished already?”
  • still in negatives goes before haven't: “I still haven't decided.”

6. Patterns examiners expect

  • “It is the first time I have visited London.” (also: the second time, the only…)
  • Superlatives: “This is the best essay I have ever written.”
  • Time clauses about the future: “I will call you after I have finished.”
  • Passive: “The results have been published.” (formal writing, Task 1/2)
  • “This is the third time this week that he has been late.”

7. British vs American use

In American English, “Did you eat yet?” and “I just saw him” are normal. In British English and most exams, use the perfect: “Have you eaten yet?” “I have just seen him.” In IELTS, both can be accepted if consistent, but the perfect is the safe choice.

8. Pronunciation and listening traps

Spoken forms: I've, you've, he's, she's, we've, haven't, hasn't. “She's finished” = she has; “She's finishing” = she is. The participle after the contraction tells you which. In Listening, hearing the weak “’ve” is often the only clue.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • The NOW test. Can you add “and it still matters / the period isn't over”? Yes → present perfect.
  • Ago = never perfect. “Two years ago”, “yesterday”, “in 2018”, “last week” are closed times. Choose past simple.
  • When did…? = past simple. “When” questions ask for a finished time, so: “When did you arrive?” not “When have you arrived?”
  • First time = perfect. “It's the first time…”, “This is the best… I've ever…” always take the perfect in the clause.
  • Elimination in MCQs. Strike every perfect option if the sentence has a closed time word. Strike every past simple option if it has “so far/yet/ever/this week”.
  • For vs since in one second. Number or length → for. Date or event → since.
  • Result clue. Look for a present trace: “I can't log in; I have forgotten my password.”
  • Writing Task 1 safety. Closed years (2005–2015) → past simple. “Over the last decade… has risen” → perfect.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “Since 2019” vs “in 2019”: Since + perfect; in + past simple.
  • “Today / this morning”: Perfect if still ongoing, past simple if the period is over: “I saw her this morning” (it's now evening).
  • “Recently / lately”: Perfect usually; past simple appears in American English.
  • Stative verbs with “for”: “I have known her for years” not “have been knowing”.
  • “How long are you here?”: Wrong: “How long have you been here?”
  • “Did you ever…?” in a gap-fill: If the options include “Have you ever”, choose it.
  • “Yet” with past simple: Avoid in exams unless the question is clearly American style.
  • “Has/Have” with collective nouns: “The government has announced” (UK often “have” for people in the group, but “has” is safe).

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Check the period: does it include now?
  • Use for/since with states and durations.
  • Use ever/never/just/already/yet in the right place.
  • Use been for visits, gone for away now.

Don't

  • Don't use it with yesterday, ago, last, in 2019, when.
  • Don't use have went; use gone.
  • Don't use has/have with the wrong subject.
  • Don't use perfect with a finished time clause.

Why we get it wrong

Same word, different tense in your first language

“I have seen him yesterday”.

Try this: Closed time word = past simple.

Participle forms not learned

have went, have wrote.

Try this: Practise participles in the table.

Not seeing the present link

Perfect for a story with dates.

Try this: Ask: why does this matter now?

Small habits that help

  • Write three sentences daily: 'I have just…'.
  • Underline all perfects in a reading passage and name the link to now.
  • In Task 1, 'has risen' needs 'over the period'; 'rose' needs years.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: experiences and achievements: “I have learned several study techniques.”
  • Writing: changes up to now: “The city has become more crowded in recent years.”
  • Reading: result/experience with no finished date.
  • Listening: catch I've / she's / haven't.

Real world: interviews and CVs — “I have managed teams for five years.”

TOEFL, PTE, GRE and bank or civil-service English tests ask the same thing: does the tense match the time you mean?

Practice

Spot the mistake

  1. She has visited Italy in 2019.
    Show answerShe visited Italy in 2019.: closed time.
  2. I live here since 2020.
    Show answerI have lived here since 2020.: since needs perfect.
  3. Have you finished your homework yesterday?
    Show answerDid you finish your homework yesterday?: yesterday closes the period.
  4. He has went home.
    Show answerHe has gone home.: participle of go.
  5. How long do you know her?
    Show answerHow long have you known her?: state continuing to now.
  6. I have seen her two days ago.
    Show answerI saw her two days ago.: ago = closed.
  7. She has been to Paris, so she isn't here.
    Show answerShe has gone to Paris, so she isn't here.: gone = still away.
  8. I have been knowing him since childhood.
    Show answerI have known him since childhood.: stative verb.

Say it another way

  1. “The last time I saw her was in May.” (not … since)
    Show answerI have not seen her since May.
  2. “This is my first visit to Japan.” (never)
    Show answerI have never been to Japan before.
  3. “Start of the job: 2021; still working.” (for)
    Show answerI have worked here for … years / since 2021.
  4. “She is not at home; she is in Dubai.” (gone)
    Show answerShe has gone to Dubai.
  5. “It is the best film. I saw it.” (ever)
    Show answerIt is the best film I have ever seen.
  6. “I started learning English in 2015 and still learn.” (since)
    Show answerI have been learning English since 2015.

Fill the gaps

I (1) ___ (live) in Dhaka since 2015. I (2) ___ (move) here after I (3) ___ (finish) school. So far I (4) ___ (visit) five countries, but I (5) ___ never (be) to Japan. Last year I (6) ___ (go) to Nepal, and it was the best trip I (7) ___ ever (take). I (8) ___ not (decide) my next trip yet.

Speak and write

  • Speaking (2 min): Describe three things you have achieved this year. Use for/since once each.
  • Writing: 120 words on how your city has changed in the last ten years. Use at least four present perfect forms and two past simple forms with years.
  • Daily habit: each evening say “Today I have…” three times, then “Yesterday I…” three times.
  • Listening drill: write down every “’ve/’s/hasn’t” you hear in a short video and decide has/is.

Making it stick

  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Before you answer, ask: when did it happen, what do I mean, and what does the verb need?
  • Redo the quiz after a day, three days and a week.
  • Read examples aloud, then write three about your own life.
