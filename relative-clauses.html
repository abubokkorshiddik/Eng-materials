Intermediate level

Relative clauses

Add detail to a noun with who, which, that, whose, where and when, instead of writing extra short sentences.

In plain words

A relative clause describes a noun. It answers the question “which one?” or gives extra detail. Instead of two short sentences (I met a man. He works at the bank.) you attach the second to the first: I met a man who works at the bank. The relative pronoun does two jobs at once. It links the clauses and it stands in for the noun, so you never repeat that noun.

How to build it

People who / that: The teacher who helped me…

Things which / that: The book which I read…

Owner whose + noun: A girl whose father is a pilot…

Place, time where / when: The city where I live. The day when we met.

When we use it

Defining: needed to identify

The student who sits near me is Ana.

No commas. Without the clause we would not know which student.

Non-defining: extra information

My brother, who lives in Dubai, is a doctor.

Commas on both sides. Use who or which, never that.

Dropping the pronoun

The film I watched was long.

You can drop who, which or that only when it is the object.

Whose

A country whose economy is growing attracts investors.

Shows possession for people, things and organisations.

Where, when, why

The reason why he left is unclear.

Place, time and reason.

Look closely at the difference

The man who called is my uncle.
The man I called is my uncle.

A subject pronoun (who) must stay. An object pronoun can go.

My sister, who lives in Delhi, is a nurse.
My sister who lives in Delhi is a nurse.

Commas suggest one sister. No commas suggest there are several.

The book which is on the desk is mine.
The book on the desk is mine.

A shorter, reduced form.

The city where I was born…
The city in which I was born…

A formal version with the preposition.

Commas change meaning in relative clauses. Learn that first.

Mistakes people make

The man who he called is my uncle. → The man whom I called is my uncle. / The man I called is my uncle.

Do not add a second pronoun.

The book which I read it was good. → The book which I read was good.

Do not repeat the object.

My brother, that lives in Dubai, is a doctor. → My brother, who lives in Dubai, is a doctor.

No that after a comma.

The people which live here are kind. → The people who live here are kind.

Use who for people.

The woman whose her son is a pilot… → The woman whose son is a pilot…

Whose already shows the owner.

I know a man, who is a doctor. → I know a man who is a doctor.

A defining clause takes no comma.

The reason because he left… → The reason why he left…

Use why after reason.

Going deeper

Core test: which noun?

Find the noun you are describing. Choose who (person), which (thing) or whose (owner). Then decide: does the clause identify the noun? Yes: no commas. No, it is extra: commas.

Who, whom, which, that

Whom is formal for the object of a person: the man whom I met. Most of the time who, that or nothing is fine. That works only in defining clauses.

Prepositions

Everyday: the person I talked to. Formal: the person to whom I talked. Never “the person to who”.

Reduced relative clauses

Active: people who live here → people living here. Passive: the book which was written by her → the book written by her. Shorter and more academic.

Which for a whole idea

He passed the exam, which surprised everyone. Here which refers to the whole situation. It needs a comma.

Agreement

The verb agrees with the noun the pronoun stands for: students who work, a student who works.

Quantity + of whom/which

She has three sisters, two of whom are doctors. The reports, most of which were old, were thrown away.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • People = who. Things = which. That works for both in defining clauses.
  • Commas mean extra. No commas mean necessary.
  • Don’t repeat the noun. No he, she or it after the relative pronoun.
  • Shorten with -ing. students studying abroad.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “That” after a comma: Never. Use which or who.
  • Whose and who’s: Whose = of whom. Who’s = who is.
  • Where already includes in: The city where I live. Not “the city where I live in”.
  • What after a noun: What means “the thing that”. Not “the book what I read”.

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Choose who, which or whose by the noun.
  • Use commas only for extra information.
  • Drop object pronouns for a natural style.
  • Join short sentences into one with a relative clause.

Don’t

  • Don’t repeat the subject or object after the pronoun.
  • Don’t use that after a comma.
  • Don’t use what after a noun.
  • Don’t say “the person to who”.

Why we get it wrong

Direct translation

In some languages the pronoun is repeated: the man that I saw him.

Try this: Check whether the pronoun already replaces the noun.

Comma confusion

Learners add commas by sound, but commas change meaning here.

Try this: Ask: if I remove the clause, can the reader still tell which one I mean?

Avoiding the structure

Short sentences feel safer, so relative clauses are skipped.

Try this: Join two sentences into one each day.

Small habits that help

  • Take two sentences about the same noun and join them.
  • Read a news article and underline every relative clause.
  • Describe objects around you with who, which and whose.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: describe people and places. “My friend who lives abroad…”
  • Writing: add detail without extra sentences. “Countries which invest in education…”
  • Reading: long sentences get simpler once you find the noun the clause describes.
  • Listening: relative pronouns often introduce the key detail.

Real world: describe a person, a product or a place clearly in one sentence.

Don’t overdo it. One or two accurate relative clauses per paragraph is plenty.

Practice

Spot the mistake

  1. The man who he called is my uncle.
    Show answerThe man I called is my uncle.: no second pronoun.
  2. My mother, that lives in Dhaka, is old.
    Show answerMy mother, who lives in Dhaka, is old.: no that after a comma.
  3. The woman whose her car was stolen called the police.
    Show answerThe woman whose car was stolen called the police.: whose already shows the owner.
  4. I know a girl, who plays the violin.
    Show answerI know a girl who plays the violin.: defining clause, no comma.

Say it another way

  1. Join: “I met a man. He works at the bank.”
    Show answerI met a man who works at the bank.
  2. Join: “London is a big city. I was born there.”
    Show answerLondon is the big city where I was born.

Fill the gaps

The woman (1) ___ lives next door is a doctor. The book (2) ___ I borrowed was good. My brother, (3) ___ lives in Dubai, is an engineer. The village (4) ___ I grew up is small. A country (5) ___ economy is growing attracts investors.

Speak and write

  • Describe three people you know using who.
  • Write six sentences about your city using where, which and who.

Making it stick

  • Each day, join two short sentences about the same person or thing.
  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Redo the quiz after a day, three days and a week.
  • Read examples aloud, then write three about your own life.
