Present perfect continuous

1. Overview

Use the present perfect continuous to emphasize an activity or process that started in the past and continues now, or has recently stopped with visible evidence.

Key principle: Choose the tense to express the relationship between the action and the time reference. Time words help, but meaning decides.

2. Form and structure

Subject + have/has + been + verb-ing

Positive

Subject + have/has + been + verb-ing

Check the auxiliary and the main verb form carefully.

Negative and questions

Put not after the first auxiliary. For questions, place the first auxiliary before the subject.

Examples: “I have not finished.” / “Have you finished?”

Build the form accurately

  • Keep the auxiliary verb that belongs to the tense.
  • Use the correct main verb form, especially irregular past participles.
  • In questions, invert the first auxiliary and the subject.
  • In negative statements, place not after the first auxiliary.

3. Main uses

Use 1

An activity continuing until now

I have been studying for two hours.

The activity began earlier and is still in progress or has only just paused.

Use 2

A recent activity with a present result

She is tired because she has been running.

The recent activity explains the present condition.

Use 3

Emphasising duration or repeated effort

They have been trying to solve the problem all morning.

The focus is on the ongoing effort, not only the result.

Use 4

Temporary ongoing situations

It has been raining since dawn.

The continuous presents the weather as an ongoing process.

Writing tip: Use the tense only when it expresses the intended time relationship. Avoid adding it to every sentence simply to sound advanced.

4. Compare the meanings

Example AExample BDifference
I have written three pages.I have been writing for three hours.The first emphasizes the completed amount or result; the second emphasizes duration and activity.
She has worked here for ten years.She has been working here for ten years.Both may be correct. The continuous emphasizes the ongoing activity; stative or long-term meanings often favour the simple.
I have read the report.I have been reading the report.The first can imply completion; the second emphasizes the process and may imply it is unfinished.

Some contexts allow more than one tense, but the viewpoint and emphasis may change. Always check what the speaker wants to communicate.

5. Time expressions

Common expressions associated with this tense include:

for, since, all day, lately, recently, how long, the whole morning

These expressions are clues, not automatic rules. The same expression can appear with different tenses when the intended meaning changes.

6. Common mistakes and explanations

Incorrect or less suitableBetter formWhy
I am studying since morning.I have been studying since morning.Use the present perfect continuous for an activity continuing from a past starting point.
She has been study all day.She has been studying all day.After been, use the -ing form.
I have been knowing him for years.I have known him for years.Know is normally stative and is not usually used in the continuous.
They has been waiting.They have been waiting.Use have with they.

Editing checklist

  • Check the auxiliary verb and subject agreement.
  • Check whether the main verb needs a base form, -ing form, or past participle.
  • Look for a finished time expression that may require a different tense.
  • Read the sentence again to make sure the tense expresses your intended meaning.

7. Apply the tense to all four IELTS skills

Speaking

Explain ongoing preparation: “I have been practising speaking every evening.”

Writing

Use it carefully to explain ongoing processes, not as a substitute for every present-tense trend.

Reading

Look for duration phrases and evidence that an activity has been continuing.

Listening

Hear the full chain have/has + been + -ing; do not confuse it with present continuous.
