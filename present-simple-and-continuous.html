Present simple and present continuous

Understand the rules, compare similar meanings, avoid common errors, and practise using both tenses in IELTS Listening, Reading, Writing, and Speaking.

1. Understand the core difference

Both tenses can refer to the present, but they present situations in different ways. The present simple often describes a fact, habit, or stable situation. The present continuous often highlights an activity in progress, a temporary situation, or a change that is developing.

Present simple

What generally happens

I read research articles every day.

This describes a routine. It does not mean that you are reading an article at this exact moment.

Present continuous

What is happening around now

I am reading a research article now.

This presents reading as an activity in progress at the current time.

Key principle: Do not choose a tense based only on a time word. Think about the meaning: fact, habit, current activity, temporary situation, or developing change.

2. Sentence structures

Present simple

Positive: Subject + base verb (add s/es for he, she, it)
Negative: Subject + do/does not + base verb
Question: Do/Does + subject + base verb?
TypeExampleWhy
PositiveShe works hard.She is third-person singular, so the verb takes -s.
NegativeShe does not work here.Does marks the third-person singular, so the main verb stays in its base form.
QuestionDoes she work here?Use does before the subject and the base verb after it.
WH-questionWhere does she work?The question word comes first, followed by does and the subject.

Rules for adding s/es

  • Most verbs take -s: work → works; read → reads.
  • Verbs ending in -s, -sh, -ch, -x, -z, and usually -o take -es: watch → watches; go → goes.
  • Consonant + y changes to -ies: study → studies; carry → carries.
  • Vowel + y takes -s: play → plays; enjoy → enjoys.
  • Have becomes has: She has a book. The verb be has special forms: I am, you/we/they are, he/she/it is.

Present continuous

Positive: Subject + am/is/are + verb-ing
Negative: Subject + am/is/are + not + verb-ing
Question: Am/Is/Are + subject + verb-ing?
TypeExampleWhy
PositiveShe is studying.Use the correct form of be plus the -ing form.
NegativeShe is not studying.Put not after the auxiliary be.
QuestionIs she studying?Move be before the subject.
WH-questionWhat is she studying?The WH-word comes first, followed by be and the subject.

Spelling rules for -ing

  • Most verbs: read → reading; work → working.
  • Drop a silent final -e: write → writing; make → making.
  • Short consonant-vowel-consonant verbs often double the final consonant: sit → sitting; run → running.
  • Verbs ending in -ie change to -ying: lie → lying; die → dying.
Common structure error: “She studying now” is incomplete because the present continuous needs an auxiliary verb. Write “She is studying now.” In contrast, “She studies every day” does not need an auxiliary in a positive present simple sentence.

3. Present simple: major rules and uses

Rule 1. General truths and scientific facts

Use the present simple for facts that are generally true or accepted as true.

Water boils at approximately 100°C at standard atmospheric pressure.

The Earth revolves around the Sun.

Plants need sunlight for photosynthesis.

Why not the continuous? These statements describe general facts, not a particular event happening now. Compare “Water boils at 100°C” (general fact) with “The water is boiling” (a particular process in progress).

IELTS use: Scientific Reading passages and Writing Task 2 arguments often use the present simple to explain established principles.

Rule 2. Habits and repeated activities

Use the present simple for routines and actions that happen regularly.

I practise English every evening.

She usually listens to podcasts on her way to university.

Many students use online resources to prepare for IELTS.

Words such as always, usually, often, sometimes, rarely, never, every day, and once a week commonly appear with habits. But the time expression is a clue, not an unbreakable rule.

Compare: “I read before bed every night” describes a routine. “I am reading more before bed these days” describes a current change in that routine.

Rule 3. Permanent or relatively stable situations

Use the present simple for situations presented as stable or long-term.

I live in Bangladesh.

She works at a university.

Dhaka is the capital of Bangladesh.

“I live in Dhaka” presents a general living situation. “I am living in Dhaka for six months” emphasizes a temporary arrangement. Permanent does not mean forever; it means the speaker presents the situation as relatively stable.

Rule 4. Schedules and timetables

Use the present simple for fixed schedules, even when the event is in the future.

The train leaves at 8:30 tomorrow morning.

The examination starts at 10 a.m.

The semester begins next month.

A timetable is presented as an established schedule. A personal arrangement more often uses the present continuous: “I am leaving at 8:30 tomorrow.” This is useful in IELTS Listening, where you may hear departure times and appointments.

Rule 5. Instructions, demonstrations, and commentary

The present simple can describe steps in a process or actions in commentary.

First, you add the water. Then, you stir the mixture.

The player passes the ball and scores.

The diagram shows how the process works.

The simple form presents the steps as a general sequence. It is common in instructions, sports commentary, and process-diagram descriptions.

Rule 6. Stative verbs

Stative verbs describe states, thoughts, feelings, possession, senses, or relationships. They are normally used in the simple form when they have a stative meaning.

MeaningCommon verbsExample
Knowledgeknow, understand, believeI understand the question.
Feelingslove, prefer, hateI prefer studying in the morning.
Possessionown, belong, possessThis book belongs to me.
Appearance or impressionseem, resembleThe answer seems correct.
Needs and desiresneed, wantI need more practice.

“I am knowing the answer” is normally incorrect because know describes a state of knowledge. However, some verbs can be stative or dynamic depending on meaning: “I think this is correct” means I believe it; “I am thinking about my future” describes a mental activity.

Rule 7. Performative verbs

Some verbs perform an action through the act of speaking, so they commonly use the present simple.

I apologise for the mistake.

I promise to finish the work.

I admit that I was wrong.

I recommend this book.

The statement itself performs the act of apologising, promising, admitting, or recommending. Other examples include accept, acknowledge, advise, deny, guarantee, inform, predict, suggest, and warn.

For a more polite or tentative expression, use a modal: “I would recommend practising all four IELTS skills.”

4. Present continuous: major rules and uses

Rule 1. Actions happening at the moment of speaking

Use the present continuous for an action in progress when you speak.

I am writing my grammar notes.

She is talking to her teacher.

They are waiting outside the classroom.

“I write notes every day” describes a habit; “I am writing notes now” describes an activity in progress.

Rule 2. Actions happening around the present

An action does not have to be happening at the exact second you speak. It can be part of your current life, study, or work.

I am reading a book about scientific research.

She is preparing for her university examination.

Researchers are developing new methods for disease prediction.

These activities may continue for days or weeks. The continuous presents them as ongoing within the current period.

Rule 3. Temporary situations

Use the continuous when a situation is temporary or is being viewed as temporary.

I am staying with my relatives this week.

She is working in another department this month.

We are using a different classroom during the renovation.

“He works at a hospital” presents a regular job. “He is working at a hospital during his placement” emphasizes a temporary arrangement. A temporary situation can last months; the viewpoint matters more than a fixed duration.

Rule 4. Changing situations and developing trends

Use the present continuous to emphasize changes in progress.

The global population is ageing.

More students are choosing online courses.

Temperatures are rising in many parts of the world.

Artificial intelligence is transforming some areas of healthcare.

“The population increases every year” emphasizes a repeated pattern. “The population is increasing rapidly” emphasizes a trend currently developing. Both forms can be correct when the intended meaning differs.

Rule 5. Repeated actions with emotional emphasis

The continuous can describe repeated behaviour that feels surprising, irritating, or noteworthy. It often appears with always, constantly, continually, or forever.

He is always interrupting me.

You are constantly checking your phone.

She is always helping other students.

“He always interrupts me” is a neutral habit statement. “He is always interrupting me” can express annoyance. The continuous can also express admiration, as in “She is always helping others.”

Rule 6. Personal future arrangements

The present continuous can describe a future plan that has already been arranged.

I am meeting my teacher tomorrow.

We are taking the IELTS test next month.

She is travelling to Dhaka on Friday.

The form presents the arrangement as established. Compare “The train leaves at 8:30” (timetable) with “I am leaving at 8:30” (personal arrangement). Not every future event needs will.

Rule 7. What is in progress at a particular time

At 9 p.m., I am usually studying.

This week, we are preparing for our final examinations.

At present, the company is expanding its operations.

“I usually study at 9 p.m.” may mean I begin studying then. “I am usually studying at 9 p.m.” suggests I am already engaged in the activity at that time.

5. Present simple versus present continuous

Exam tip: Both tenses may be grammatically possible in some contexts. Choose the form that accurately communicates the meaning, rather than memorising one tense for each time word.

Three extra mistakes to watch for

  • “He always is interrupting me.” More natural: “He is always interrupting me.” The adverb normally follows the auxiliary in this pattern.
  • “The Earth is revolving around the Sun.” For a standard scientific fact, use “The Earth revolves around the Sun.”
  • “The train is leaving at 8:30 according to the timetable.” Usually write “The train leaves at 8:30.” The simple form is conventional for fixed schedules.

8. Apply the grammar to all four IELTS skills

🗣️

Speaking

Use the simple for routines, preferences, and general opinions. Use the continuous for current projects, temporary arrangements, and changes.

Sample: “I usually read in the evening. At the moment, I am preparing for IELTS, so I am spending more time listening to English.”

The first sentence describes a routine; the other sentences focus on current activity and a temporary change.

✍️

Writing

Use the simple for general claims and what charts or diagrams show. Use the continuous to emphasize current trends or developing changes.

Examples: “The chart shows annual sales.” “More cities are investing in public transport.”

The first describes the content of a visual; the second highlights an ongoing trend. Historical data generally require past-tense forms for completed periods.

📖

Reading

Notice whether an author is stating a general fact, describing an ongoing study, or presenting a developing trend.

Examples: “Regular exercise improves health.” “Scientists are investigating new treatments.”

The first states a general relationship; the second describes research in progress. For True/False/Not Given, answers must still be supported by the passage.

🎧

Listening

Listen for auxiliary verbs, -s endings, -ing forms, time expressions, and context. These help distinguish routines, current activities, and arrangements.

Example: “When does the lecture start?” “It starts at ten, but I am meeting my tutor at nine.”

The lecture time is a schedule; the meeting is a personal arrangement. In fast speech, contractions such as “I'm” and “they're” may be reduced.

9. Advanced rules and exceptions

A. Verbs that can be stative or dynamic

VerbPresent simplePresent continuous
ThinkI think this is correct. (opinion)I am thinking about the question. (mental activity)
HaveShe has a car. (possession)She is having lunch. (activity)
SeeI see your point. (understand)I am seeing my doctor tomorrow. (meeting)
TasteThe soup tastes salty. (property)The chef is tasting the soup. (action)
SmellThe flowers smell lovely. (property)She is smelling the flowers. (action)
LookYou look tired. (appearance)She is looking at the screen. (action)
BeHe is rude. (general characteristic)He is being rude. (behaviour now)
FeelI feel tired. (state)I am feeling the fabric. (physical action)

The tense can change because the meaning changes. A state or property often takes the simple form; an activity or temporary behaviour may take the continuous.

B. Future time clauses

Use the present simple after time words such as when, before, after, until, and as soon as when the clause refers to the future.

I will call you when I arrive.

We will begin after the teacher comes.

I will wait until she finishes.

Avoid “I will call you when I will arrive.” English normally uses the present simple in this type of future time clause.

C. Passive voice

Present simple passive

am/is/are + past participle

English is spoken in many countries.

The data are collected annually.

Use this for general processes or facts where the receiver of the action is the focus.

Present continuous passive

am/is/are + being + past participle

The road is being repaired.

New methods are being developed.

Being marks the passive action as in progress.

D. Frequency adverb position

  • Before the main verb: “I often read academic papers.”
  • After the verb be: “She is usually punctual.”
  • Commonly after the auxiliary in the continuous: “He is always complaining.”

Word order can vary for emphasis, but these are reliable patterns for IELTS learners.

E. Temporary does not mean very short

“I am studying statistics” may describe a course lasting several years. The continuous presents studying as an ongoing activity in the current period. Temporary versus permanent is a useful guideline, not an absolute rule.

11. Error correction practice

Try correcting each sentence before opening the answer key.

  1. She don't understand the question.
  2. I am believing that education is important.
  3. More students is choosing online learning nowadays.
  4. He is working here every day as part of his permanent job.
  5. The bus is arriving at 8 a.m. according to the official timetable.
  6. I am studying English since January.
  7. The researcher are investigating the causes of the outbreak.
  8. I will call you when I will finish my work.
  9. She is always arrive late for class.
  10. The chart is showing the percentage of people who use public transport.
  1. She doesn't understand the question. Use doesn't + base verb with she.
  2. I believe that education is important. Believe is normally stative when expressing an opinion.
  3. More students are choosing online learning nowadays. Students is plural; the continuous highlights a developing trend.
  4. He works here every day as part of his permanent job. The simple describes a regular, stable situation.
  5. The bus arrives at 8 a.m. according to the official timetable. The simple is standard for scheduled arrivals.
  6. I have been studying English since January. The present perfect continuous describes an activity that started in the past and continues now.
  7. The researchers are investigating the causes of the outbreak. Researchers is plural, so use are.
  8. I will call you when I finish my work. Use the present simple in this future time clause.
  9. She always arrives late for class. For a neutral habit, use the simple. “She is always arriving late” can express irritation.
  10. The chart shows the percentage of people who use public transport. The simple is the usual choice for what a chart presents.

12. Final revision checklist

  • Is it a general fact, habit, or stable situation? Consider the present simple.
  • Is the action in progress or part of a current activity? Consider the present continuous.
  • Is it a current trend or temporary arrangement? The continuous may be more suitable.
  • Is it a fixed timetable? The present simple is usually appropriate.
  • Is the verb stative, such as know, believe, or understand? The simple form is normally used for its stative meaning.
  • Does the sentence describe a completed past event or an activity continuing since the past? You may need another tense.
  • Check subject-verb agreement, auxiliary verbs, verb forms, and word order.
  • For IELTS, practise recognising the forms in reading and listening as well as producing them in speaking and writing.
Study method: Learn the rule, compare examples, listen for the forms in natural speech, read them in context, and then create your own sentences. Accuracy across all four skills comes from understanding meaning, not only memorising rules.
