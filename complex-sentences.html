Intermediate level

Complex sentences

A complex sentence joins a main idea to a supporting idea with the right connector.

In plain words

A simple sentence says one thing. A complex sentence says one main thing and tells you how a second idea relates to it: why, when, despite what, under what condition. The connector does the work. Choose the wrong one and the logic breaks. Choose the right one and your writing sounds clear and mature. The main clause can stand alone. The supporting clause cannot.

How to build it

Pattern 1 Main clause + connector + clause: I stayed home because it rained.

Pattern 2 Connector + clause, main clause: Because it rained, I stayed home.

Compound Main clause, and/but/so + main clause: It rained, so I stayed home.

Contrast Although it rained, we went out. (never although … but)

When we use it

Reason

He left early because he felt ill.

Because, since and as answer why.

Time

I will call you when I arrive.

Use the present tense after time words, even for the future.

Contrast

Although the plan was cheap, it failed.

Although, though, even though, whereas and while show an unexpected or opposite result.

Condition

Unless you hurry, you will be late.

Unless means if not.

Purpose and result

The test was so hard that many students failed.

So that, so…that and such…that show goal or result.

Pairs that cause trouble

Because it was late, we left.
It was late, so we left.

Both are fine, but never together: not “Because … so …”.

Although it was cold, we swam.
It was cold, but we swam.

Never “Although … but”.

Despite the rain, we went out.
Despite the fact that it rained, we went out.

Despite takes a noun, an -ing form, or “the fact that”.

While I was cooking, he called.
I was cooking, while he was reading.

Time vs contrast: the same word, two jobs.

Simple sentences are not bad. IELTS rewards a mix. Use a complex sentence where the ideas really connect.

Mistakes people make

Because it was raining, so we stayed. → Because it was raining, we stayed.

One connector for one relationship.

Although he was tired, but he continued. → Although he was tired, he continued.

Not although and but together.

Despite of the rain, we went. → Despite the rain, we went.

No of after despite. In spite of keeps its of.

I was late, I missed the bus. → I was late because I missed the bus.

Comma splice: two clauses need a connector.

I left because I was tired. Because the room was hot. → I left because I was tired and the room was hot.

Do not leave a because-clause on its own.

If I will see him, I will tell him. → If I see him, I will tell him.

Present tense after if.

He is rich whereas he is happy. → He is rich but not happy.

Whereas compares two different things.

Going deeper

Core test: can it stand alone?

Take the clause that starts with the connector. If it cannot stand alone, it is the supporting clause and needs a main clause.

Two kinds of linking

And, but, so, or and yet join equal ideas, usually with a comma: It rained, so we stayed. Because, although, if, when and while make one clause depend on the other.

Comma rules

Supporting clause first: comma after it. Although it rained, we went. Main clause first: usually no comma. We went although it rained.

Same subject shortcuts

If both clauses share a subject, you can use -ing: After finishing the task, I went home. The subject must match. “Walking home, the rain started” is wrong.

Clause or noun?

Because, although and while take a clause. Because of, despite, in spite of and due to take a noun or an -ing form: because of the rain, despite working hard.

Whereas and while for comparing

Urban areas are crowded, whereas rural areas are quiet. Very useful for comparing two items in Task 1 and Task 2.

Don’t overuse linking words

Starting every sentence with Moreover or Furthermore sounds mechanical. Connect ideas inside the sentence with because, although and which.

Shortcuts that work in exams

  • One idea, one connector. Never two linking words for one relationship.
  • Although means but. So don’t write both.
  • Check the comma. Supporting clause first means a comma after it.
  • Clause or noun? because / although + clause. because of / despite + noun or -ing.

Where exams try to catch you

  • “Since”: Reason (Since it was late) or time (since 2020). Check which one you mean.
  • “While”: Time (While I was reading) or contrast (While some agree, others do not).
  • “Though”, “although”, “even though”: Even though is stronger. Though can also end a spoken sentence: It was cheap. Not good, though.
  • “As”: Can mean because, when or while. Use because when you want to be clear.

What to do, and what to leave alone

Do

  • Choose the connector for the relationship: reason, time, contrast, condition.
  • Put a comma after an opening supporting clause.
  • Mix short and longer sentences.
  • Check every sentence has a main clause.

Don’t

  • Don’t use although with but, or because with so.
  • Don’t join two clauses with only a comma.
  • Don’t use will after if, when or before.
  • Don’t write a because-clause alone as a sentence.

Why we get it wrong

Translation habit

Some languages pair connectors, so “although … but” feels natural.

Try this: Learn each connector as a whole pattern with an example.

Fear of mistakes

Learners avoid complex sentences and write many short ones.

Try this: Start by joining two short sentences with because or although.

Mixing up grammar roles

Despite and because of need nouns. Although and because need clauses.

Try this: Mark each one “noun” or “clause” in your notes.

Small habits that help

  • Take five short sentences from your writing and join them into two or three.
  • Keep a list of connectors sorted by job: reason, contrast, time, condition.
  • Read an article and circle every connector.

In IELTS and real life

  • Speaking: extend answers with because, although and which. “I like cities because there is so much to do.”
  • Writing: use a variety. Complex sentences count towards Grammatical Range and Accuracy.
  • Reading: connectors show the writer’s logic: contrast, cause, result.
  • Listening: but, although and however often signal a change of direction.

Real world: emails and reports need clear logic: because, so that, although.

Accuracy matters more than complexity. One correct complex sentence beats three broken ones.

Practice

Spot the mistake

  1. Although it rained, but we went out.
    Show answerAlthough it rained, we went out.: one connector only.
  2. Despite of the cost, I bought it.
    Show answerDespite the cost, I bought it.: no of after despite.
  3. I was late, I missed the bus.
    Show answerI was late because I missed the bus.: comma splice.
  4. If it will rain, we stay home.
    Show answerIf it rains, we will stay home.: present tense after if.

Say it another way

  1. Join with although: “It was cheap. It was poor quality.”
    Show answerAlthough it was cheap, it was poor quality.
  2. Join with because: “I stayed home. I was ill.”
    Show answerI stayed home because I was ill.

Fill the gaps

(1) ___ it was raining, we went out. I will call you (2) ___ I arrive. He failed (3) ___ he worked hard. (4) ___ you hurry, you will miss the train. (5) ___ of the noise, I could not sleep.

Speak and write

  • Talk for one minute about your city using because, although and which.
  • Take a paragraph of short sentences you wrote and rewrite it with three complex sentences.

Making it stick

  • Each day, join two short sentences with a connector, and say why you chose it.
  • Keep a small notebook of your own mistakes. Write the wrong sentence, the right one, and why. Read it every other day.
  • Redo the quiz after a day, three days and a week.
  • Read examples aloud, then write three about your own life.
